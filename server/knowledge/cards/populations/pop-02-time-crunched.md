## Summary
Most amateur cyclists have 3–6 hours a week and a life that regularly takes some of it away. Good fitness is achievable on that: intensity is potent per minute, and consistency over months matters more than weekly hours. The limits are real too: durability for long events is hard to build without some long rides, and recovery capacity is shared with work and family stress. The plan should be built around what is reliably available.

## Key numbers
- Effective minimum: 3 sessions per week, 2 of them with intensity, about 3–4 h total; noticeable gains for 6–12 months from a modest base.
- Session formats that fit 45–60 min: 10 min warm-up + 4–5 × 4 min VO2max; 3 × 10–12 min threshold; 2 × 20 min sweet spot; 3 sets of 8–10 × 30/15 s.
- 30-min option: 8 min warm-up, 3 × 5 min at 100–105% FTP or 2 sets of 6 × 30/15 s, short cool-down.
- Long ride: one of 2–3 h every 1–2 weeks keeps basic durability; for long events, prioritise it in the last 8 weeks.
- Occasional volume: a big weekend or a few days of riding 2–4 times a year gives a useful stimulus.
- Hard sessions: still no more than 2–3 per week; more does not fit recovery when sleep and stress are constrained.

## In practice
- Schedule sessions like appointments; early mornings and indoor training are the most protectable slots.
- Commutes are free volume (see `pop-09-ebike-commuting`).
- Have a default short session ready for days that collapse.
- In heavy life weeks, keep frequency and drop intensity.
- Set goals that match the hours: fast group rides, shorter events and climbing goals suit low volume better than ultra events.
- Missed sessions are dropped, not made up.

## Limits
Evidence for low-volume interval training comes mostly from less-trained subjects and short studies; long-term plateaus are common without periods of higher volume.

## Coach guidance
Never imply that more time is required to be a "real" cyclist. Build from the rider's actual schedule and protect sleep and family time in the advice.
