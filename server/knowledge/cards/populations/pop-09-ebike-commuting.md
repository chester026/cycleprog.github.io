## Summary
Riding to work and riding with electric assistance are both real exercise. Commuting adds low-intensity volume that would otherwise not exist and is associated with clear health benefits in population studies. Pedal-assist e-bikes lower the effort for a given speed, yet reviews show riders still work at moderate intensity, enough to improve fitness in inactive people. For a training cyclist, commutes are useful easy hours and e-bikes are a tool for recovery days, for riding with faster partners, for returning from injury or illness, and for hilly routes that would otherwise be too hard.

## Key numbers
- E-bike riding: typically moderate intensity, around 3–6 METs, with heart rate somewhat lower than on a conventional bike at the same speed; on e-mountain bikes, riders in one study still averaged over 90% of the heart rate they reached on conventional bikes.
- Commuting: 2 × 30 min, 4 days a week adds 4 h of LOW volume.
- A 30–45 min commute can hold a structured element once or twice a week, e.g. 2–3 × 6–8 min tempo or a few 10–20 s sprints, traffic permitting.
- Fatigue cost: daily commutes at easy effort add little; ridden hard every day they become unplanned MID work (see `tid-10-moderate-intensity-rut`).
- Most e-bike motors assist up to 25 km/h (EU) or 32–45 km/h (some regions/classes); above that the rider does all the work on a heavier bike.

## In practice
- Record commutes so load tracking is complete; without a power meter, use duration and RPE.
- Keep most commutes truly easy; let planned sessions carry the intensity.
- Choose assistance level to keep effort conversational on recovery days.
- For mixed-ability couples and groups, an e-bike equalises pace and keeps everyone riding together.
- Safety and visibility in traffic come before any training goal: lights, predictable riding, no intervals in busy streets.
- E-bike power data usually exclude or mix motor output; interpret with heart rate and RPE.

## Limits
Health evidence is strongest for inactive adults; little research covers e-bikes within structured training.

## Coach guidance
Count these rides as legitimate training and never rank them below "real" rides. Help place them so they add easy volume without eroding recovery.
