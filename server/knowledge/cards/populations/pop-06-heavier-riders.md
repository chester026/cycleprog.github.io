## Summary
Body size shapes where a rider is fast. Larger riders usually produce more absolute power, and because air resistance grows more slowly than body mass, they have an advantage on flat and rolling roads, in time trials, into headwinds and on descents. On steep climbs, where power per kilogram decides, they work harder for the same speed. Good pacing, gearing and equipment choices let heavier riders enjoy and succeed in every kind of riding; the body they have is the one to train.

## Key numbers
- Frontal area scales with roughly the two-thirds power of body mass: a rider 20% heavier has only about 12–13% more drag area but often 15–20% more absolute power.
- Flat riding at 35 km/h: an 85 kg rider and a 65 kg rider with the same W/kg are not equal; the heavier rider is clearly faster.
- Climbing at 8%: speed follows W/kg of total system mass almost directly.
- Gearing: plan for a lowest gear at or below 1:1 for sustained gradients above 7–8% (see `bike-10-gearing`).
- Tyres: wider (28–32 mm+) with pressure raised for load, roughly +0.2–0.3 bar per 10 kg over the reference tables.
- Equipment limits: many lightweight wheels, seatposts and saddles have rider or system weight limits (often 100–120 kg); check before buying.
- Heat: larger bodies produce more heat and shed it less easily; hydration and cooling matter more on hot climbs.

## In practice
- On climbs, ride one's own power from the bottom; regroup or chase on flats and descents where the advantage returns.
- In groups, heavier riders are valued on the front in wind and on flats.
- Events with rolling terrain, time trials and flat fondos play to strengths.
- Comfort: saddle choice and width, chamois quality, bar width and a professional fit pay off quickly.
- Strength is often a natural asset: sprinting and short power can be very good.
- Any discussion of body weight is the rider's to start (see `nut-19-weight-management`).

## Limits
Scaling relationships are averages; aerodynamics depends on shape and position as much as size.

## Coach guidance
Never frame size as a problem. Use absolute watts alongside W/kg, choose goals that fit, and give practical gearing and pacing advice. Do not bring up weight loss unless the rider asks.
