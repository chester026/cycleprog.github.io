## Summary
Someone who trained seriously in the past and returns after years is neither a beginner nor the athlete they were. Skills, pacing sense and knowledge remain, and fitness returns faster than it was first built. The cardiovascular system and motivation come back quickly; tendons, joints, contact points and, for older returners, recovery capacity lag behind. The main risk is training by memory: old volumes, old paces and old zones.

## Key numbers
- After several years off, VO2max and threshold return to near age-adjusted values for an active person; expect a starting FTP well below old bests.
- Regain: commonly 60–80% of the gap to a realistic current potential within 3–6 months of consistent training; the remainder over 1–2 years.
- Start: 3–4 rides per week, mostly LOW, 60–70% of what feels possible; increase weekly time by about 10–15%.
- Hard sessions: after 4–6 weeks; 1 per week at first.
- Age adjustment: about 5–10% per decade lower sustainable power than at peak for a similarly trained state; maximal heart rate lower by about 7 beats per decade, so old heart-rate zones are invalid.
- Test after 3–4 weeks of riding, then every 6–8 weeks as fitness moves quickly.
- Medical check advisable after a long sedentary period, especially over 40 or with risk factors.

## In practice
- Retire old personal bests as benchmarks for now; create a "comeback" set of bests.
- Old injuries tend to resurface: rebuild with strength and mobility work alongside riding.
- Bike fit may need to change with flexibility and body shape.
- Equipment and training methods have moved on (wider tyres, lower pressures, higher carbohydrate intake); update habits.
- Use experience as an asset: pacing, group skills and race sense usually survive.
- Expect rapid early progress, then a slower phase; both are normal.

## Limits
Evidence on long-term retention of training adaptations in humans is limited; guidance is experience-based.

## Coach guidance
Acknowledge the rider's history with respect, then anchor every target in current data. Frame the comeback as its own project with visible progress, not as a deficit against the past.
