## Summary
The gut adapts to what it is asked to do. Regularly taking in carbohydrate and fluid during training increases stomach comfort, speeds emptying and raises absorption capacity, so higher race-day intakes are tolerated with fewer symptoms. Riders who train on water and then take 90 g/h in a race are testing an untrained system.

## Key numbers
- Two weeks of daily gut-challenge during exercise reduced gut symptoms and malabsorption and improved performance in a controlled trial with runners.
- Practical progression: start at 40–60 g/h on long rides; add about 10–15 g/h every 1–2 weeks; hold each level for 2–3 long sessions.
- Reaching 90 g/h comfortably typically takes 4–8 weeks; 100–120 g/h longer.
- Practise at race intensity at least 2–3 times: gut blood flow falls as intensity rises.
- Daily diet matters: a habitually higher-carbohydrate diet increases intestinal transporter capacity.

## In practice
- Use the products and schedule planned for the event.
- Common causes of race-day gut trouble: too much at once, too concentrated, too little fluid with gels, high fibre/fat/protein in the pre-race meal, heat and dehydration, anti-inflammatory painkillers, nerves.
- If symptoms start: reduce intensity slightly, switch to small sips, resume intake in smaller doses when settled.
- In the 24 h before an important event, lower fibre and fat intake.
- Some riders tolerate solids poorly at high intensity; keep solids for easier sections.

## Limits
Most direct evidence comes from runners, who have more gut trouble than cyclists. Persistent or severe symptoms (blood, pain, symptoms outside exercise) need medical assessment.

## Coach guidance
Treat fuelling as a trainable skill with its own progression in the plan. Never recommend a new high intake for the first time on event day.
