## Summary
Ketone esters raise blood ketone levels within minutes without a ketogenic diet. They became famous through professional cycling, but the research does not support the reputation. Taken before or during exercise, they do not improve performance on average and sometimes impair it, partly through gut discomfort and a mild acid load. A separate line of research suggests possible benefits when taken after exercise during very heavy training blocks; that evidence comes from few studies and has not been consistently confirmed.

## Key numbers
- Meta-analysis of randomized trials: no significant effect of acute ketone supplementation on endurance performance.
- One early study reported about 2% improvement in a 30-min time trial following prolonged riding; several later studies found no effect or a decrement of 1–3%.
- Typical dose in studies: 25–50 g of ketone ester, raising blood beta-hydroxybutyrate to about 2–4 mmol/L.
- Post-exercise use in a 3-week overload study: better tolerance of the training load and higher voluntary energy intake were reported (one research group).
- Cost: several euros or dollars per serving; taste is strongly bitter.
- Ketone salts raise ketones much less and deliver a large mineral load.

## In practice
- For amateurs there is no performance case: the same money and attention yield far more when spent on carbohydrate, sleep and coaching.
- Ketones do not replace carbohydrate during hard riding.
- Riders who want to experiment should do so in training first and expect possible nausea.

## Limits
Formulations and protocols differ between studies; long-term effects are unknown; the recovery and adaptation claims need independent replication.

## Coach guidance
Answer questions about ketones factually: popular, expensive, not supported for performance. Do not recommend them.
