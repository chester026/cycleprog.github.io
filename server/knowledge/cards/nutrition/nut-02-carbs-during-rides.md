## Summary
Eating carbohydrate while riding maintains blood glucose, supplies fuel as glycogen falls and improves performance in any ride longer than about an hour. The amount scales with duration and intensity. Classic guidelines top out at 90 g/h; professionals now commonly take 100–120 g/h. Lab data show that 120 g/h raises the amount of ingested carbohydrate burned but does not spare the body's own stores more than 90 g/h, and a performance advantage over 90 g/h is not yet established.

## Key numbers
| Duration | Carbohydrate |
|---|---|
| Under 45–60 min | none needed |
| 1–2 h | 30–60 g/h |
| 2–3 h | 60–90 g/h |
| 3 h+ or racing | 80–100 g/h; up to 120 g/h if gut-trained |

- Above about 60 g/h a glucose + fructose mix is required.
- In highly trained cyclists riding 3 h, 120 g/h (fructose:maltodextrin 0.8:1) gave exogenous oxidation of about 1.5 g/min versus 1.3 g/min at 90 g/h (1:2); use of the body's own carbohydrate was the same.
- One gel ≈ 20–30 g (up to 40–45 g for large gels); 500 ml sports drink ≈ 30–40 g (high-carb mixes 80–90 g); a banana ≈ 25 g; a bar ≈ 25–45 g.

## In practice
- Start in the first 20–30 min and take small amounts every 15–20 min.
- Easy rides under 90 min need nothing; long LOW rides are still better fuelled at 40–60 g/h.
- Higher intakes also speed recovery for the next day, which matters in training blocks and stage events.
- Smaller riders and lower power outputs need the lower part of each range.

## Limits
Individual oxidation capacity and gut tolerance vary widely; intake beyond what the gut absorbs causes bloating and diarrhoea.

## Coach guidance
Give a number in g/h for the specific ride and translate it into products or foods. Ask what the rider actually took when late-ride power fades.
