## Summary
Alcohol affects training mainly through recovery and sleep. Large amounts after exercise reduce muscle protein synthesis, slow rehydration, displace the food needed for refuelling, and fragment sleep; next-day performance and perceived effort suffer. Small amounts with a meal have little measurable effect on performance. The dose, the timing relative to hard sessions and the frequency are what matter.

## Key numbers
- A heavy dose after exercise (about 1.5 g/kg, roughly 8–12 standard drinks for a 72 kg person) reduced muscle protein synthesis by 24% when taken with protein and by 37% when taken with carbohydrate only.
- Sleep: alcohol shortens sleep onset but disrupts the second half of the night and reduces REM sleep; overnight HRV is suppressed and resting heart rate raised, visibly in wearable data, even after 2–3 drinks.
- Rehydration: drinks above about 4% alcohol increase urine output; beer-strength drinks rehydrate poorly compared with water plus sodium.
- Energy: 7 kcal per gram; a pint of beer or large glass of wine about 180–230 kcal.
- Performance the morning after heavy drinking: reduced endurance capacity and coordination, higher heart rate and RPE.

## In practice
- After key sessions and races, eat and rehydrate first; if drinking, keep it moderate and with food.
- Avoid alcohol on the nights before important sessions and events; the night two days before matters too.
- Expect lower readiness scores after drinking; that is a real physiological signal, not an error.
- For riders managing weight, alcohol is often the easiest energy to reduce.
- Never ride or drive after drinking.

## Limits
Most research uses large doses in young men; effects of moderate, habitual intake on endurance adaptation are not well studied. Individual tolerance and health risks vary.

## Coach guidance
Share the facts without judgement when the rider asks or when data clearly show the pattern. Do not moralise. Signs of dependence or drinking to cope are a reason to suggest professional help.
