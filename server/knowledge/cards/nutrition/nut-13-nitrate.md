## Summary
Nitrate from beetroot juice and leafy vegetables is converted, with the help of mouth bacteria, to nitric oxide, which can lower the oxygen cost of submaximal exercise and improve muscle function. The effect on performance is small and depends on fitness: recreational and moderately trained riders tend to benefit, while highly trained athletes often show little or nothing. It is safe as food and inexpensive to try.

## Key numbers
- Dose: 5–9 mmol nitrate (about 310–560 mg), i.e. 1–2 concentrated beetroot shots (70 ml each) or about 500 ml juice.
- Timing: 2–3 h before; effects are larger after 3–7 days of daily intake.
- Reported effects: 1–3% lower oxygen cost at a given power; time-trial improvements of roughly 1–3% in non-elite subjects; small or absent in athletes with VO2max above about 60–65 mL/kg/min.
- Best-supported uses: efforts of about 5–30 min, repeated high-intensity efforts, and exercise at altitude.
- Antibacterial mouthwash blocks the conversion and removes the effect.
- Harmless side-effects: red urine and stools.

## In practice
- Trial it before a test or a hard group ride, not first on race day; some people get stomach upset.
- A diet rich in spinach, rocket, lettuce and beetroot supplies comparable nitrate.
- Use concentrated shots with stated nitrate content; ordinary juices vary widely.
- Worth considering for recreational riders targeting climbs or time trials; low priority compared with fuelling and caffeine.

## Limits
Individual response is variable and many studies are small; publication bias toward positive findings is likely. Nitrate salts are not the same as vegetable sources and should not be self-dosed.

## Coach guidance
Present as optional and modest. Do not suggest it will compensate for training or fuelling gaps.
