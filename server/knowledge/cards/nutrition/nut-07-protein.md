## Summary
Endurance training raises protein needs above those of inactive people: amino acids are oxidised during long rides and are needed to remodel muscle and build mitochondria. Most cyclists who eat enough total food already meet the target; shortfalls are common in riders who are restricting energy, in vegetarians and vegans who do not plan for it, and at breakfast and after rides. Higher intakes help preserve muscle during weight loss and in older riders.

## Key numbers
- Daily: 1.2–2.0 g/kg; for regular training about 1.6–1.8 g/kg (72 kg rider: 115–130 g).
- Per meal: 0.3–0.4 g/kg (20–40 g), 3–5 times a day, including after training.
- Masters riders (50+): aim for the upper per-meal dose (0.4 g/kg; about 30–40 g) because muscle responds less to small doses.
- During energy restriction: 1.8–2.4 g/kg to protect lean mass.
- Before sleep: 30–40 g of slow protein (e.g. quark, yoghurt) can raise overnight muscle protein synthesis.
- 25 g protein ≈ 100 g chicken or fish, 4 eggs, 250 g Greek yoghurt or quark, 300 g tofu, 150 g cooked lentils + 60 g cheese.

## In practice
- Spread intake across the day rather than loading it into dinner.
- Plant-based riders: combine sources (legumes, soy, grains), eat slightly more total protein, and consider a soy or pea-rice protein powder for convenience.
- Protein during rides is unnecessary when carbohydrate intake is adequate; small amounts may help in ultra events.
- Protein supplements are food in convenient form, not a requirement.

## Limits
Optimal intakes for endurance adaptation (as opposed to strength) are less well studied. Riders with kidney disease need medical guidance on protein.

## Coach guidance
Check distribution (breakfast and post-ride) before total. Do not push supplements.
