## Summary
Iron and vitamin D are the two micronutrient shortfalls that most plausibly affect endurance athletes. Iron is needed for haemoglobin and for mitochondrial enzymes; deficiency causes fatigue and reduced performance, especially when it progresses to anaemia. Vitamin D supports bone, muscle and immune function; low levels are common in winter and in people who train indoors. Both are diagnosed by blood test and treated under medical guidance; supplementing blindly is ineffective at best and, for iron, potentially harmful.

## Key numbers
- Iron status: ferritin below about 30–35 µg/L is commonly treated as deficient in athletes (some practitioners act below 50 µg/L before altitude training); anaemia is defined by low haemoglobin.
- Higher risk: menstruating women, vegetarians and vegans, heavy training loads, low energy intake, frequent blood donors, adolescents.
- Iron absorption is reduced for several hours after hard exercise (hepcidin response) and by coffee, tea and calcium taken with it; vitamin C improves it.
- Dietary sources: red meat, liver, shellfish (well absorbed); legumes, tofu, leafy greens, fortified cereals (less well absorbed).
- Vitamin D: blood 25(OH)D below 30 nmol/L deficient; 50 nmol/L generally considered sufficient; many sports physicians aim for 75+ nmol/L.
- At latitudes above about 35–40°, skin production is negligible in winter months.

## In practice
- Unexplained fatigue, falling performance, breathlessness on usual efforts, frequent illness or stress fractures are reasons to see a doctor and get blood work, not to buy supplements.
- An annual blood check is reasonable for riders in a higher-risk group.
- Multivitamins do not correct a real deficiency.

## Limits
Thresholds for "optimal" ferritin and vitamin D in athletes are debated. Iron overload is dangerous; some people absorb iron excessively (haemochromatosis).

## Coach guidance
Never recommend iron doses. Suggest medical testing when the pattern fits, and continue with easier training until results are in.
