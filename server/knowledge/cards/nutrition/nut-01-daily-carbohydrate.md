## Summary
Carbohydrate needs follow the training load of the day, not a fixed percentage of calories. The principle is "fuel for the work required": more on days with long or hard sessions, less on rest and easy days. Chronic under-supply of carbohydrate is one of the commonest reasons for stalled training, poor interval quality and frequent illness in amateur cyclists.

## Key numbers (g per kg body mass per day)
| Day type | Carbohydrate |
|---|---|
| Rest or under 1 h easy | 3–5 |
| About 1 h moderate | 5–7 |
| 1–3 h with intensity | 6–10 |
| 4–5 h+ or stage racing | 8–12 |

- Example, 72 kg rider: easy day 220–360 g; 2 h with intervals 430–720 g; 5 h ride 580–860 g.
- Carbohydrate eaten on the bike counts toward the daily total.
- Glycogen restoration takes about 24 h at 7–10 g/kg; partial at lower intakes.

## In practice
- Before and after quality sessions and long rides, make carbohydrate the centre of the meal; on rest days shift toward vegetables, protein and fats.
- Whole-food sources for main meals (rice, potatoes, oats, pasta, bread, fruit); faster sources around training.
- Signs of too little: heavy legs for days, intervals fading, strong evening hunger, irritability, poor sleep.
- Riders who train before work need carbohydrate the evening before and during the session.

## Limits
Ranges are wide because needs depend on intensity, body size and goals; they come from glycogen studies and consensus rather than long-term outcome trials. Lower intakes are sometimes used deliberately (see `nut-08-fasted-low-glycogen-training`).

## Coach guidance
Give carbohydrate guidance per day type in grams and in food terms. Do not give restrictive targets to riders who describe under-eating, weight preoccupation or missed periods; point them to `nut-20-energy-availability-reds` guidance.
