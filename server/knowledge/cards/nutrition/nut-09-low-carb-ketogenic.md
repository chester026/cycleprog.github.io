## Summary
A ketogenic diet (very low carbohydrate, high fat) can double the rate at which trained athletes burn fat within a few weeks. It does not improve endurance performance in controlled trials, and it impairs performance at higher intensities: fat needs more oxygen per unit of energy, and the capacity to use carbohydrate quickly is down-regulated. Since nearly all cycling events are decided by efforts at or above threshold, a high-carbohydrate approach remains the evidence-based default.

## Key numbers
- Ketogenic: under about 50 g carbohydrate per day, 75–80% of energy from fat.
- Fat oxidation: rises from about 0.5–0.7 to 1.2–1.5 g/min after 3–4 weeks of adaptation.
- Oxygen cost at race pace rose and performance gains from training were lost in elite race walkers on the ketogenic diet, while high- and periodized-carbohydrate groups improved 10 km times by about 5–7%.
- Carbohydrate yields about 5–7% more energy per litre of oxygen than fat.
- Adaptation period: 2–4 weeks of reduced training capacity.
- Restoring carbohydrate for 1–2 days before racing does not fully restore high-intensity capacity.

## Where it may fit
Very long, steady, low-intensity events where gut tolerance of carbohydrate is the main limiter; riders who choose it for personal or medical reasons under professional supervision.

## In practice
- Riders on low-carb diets should expect lower power above threshold and should not plan HIGH-intensity blocks on it.
- A moderate approach (lower carbohydrate on rest days, high around hard sessions) keeps the benefits of both without the cost.
- Weight often drops quickly at first from glycogen and water, not fat.

## Limits
Some individuals report feeling and performing well in ultra-endurance on low-carb diets; long-term trials in cyclists are lacking. Medical conditions (e.g. diabetes) change the picture and need a doctor.

## Coach guidance
State the evidence plainly without moralising about the rider's diet choice. Adjust training expectations if they continue with it.
