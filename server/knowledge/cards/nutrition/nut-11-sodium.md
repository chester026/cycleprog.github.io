## Summary
Sodium is the main electrolyte lost in sweat and the only one worth replacing during exercise. Losses vary several-fold between people. For rides under 2–3 hours the sodium in normal food covers it. In long, hot events sodium in drinks helps retain fluid, maintains the drive to drink and makes up large losses. The dangerous condition, exercise-associated hyponatremia, is caused mainly by drinking too much fluid, not by lack of salt.

## Key numbers
- Sweat sodium: about 20–80 mmol/L, i.e. 460–1840 mg per litre; most people 700–1200 mg/L.
- Practical intake when sweating heavily for 3 h+: 300–700 mg sodium per hour (higher for salty sweaters in heat), ideally in the drink: about 500–1000 mg per litre.
- 1 g table salt contains about 390 mg sodium.
- Typical sports drinks: 250–500 mg/L; electrolyte tablets 250–500 mg each.
- Signs of a salty sweater: white stains on clothing and helmet straps, stinging eyes.
- Hyponatremia risk factors: drinking more than sweat losses for many hours, slow pace in long events, low body mass, weight gain during the event. Symptoms: bloating, nausea, headache, confusion; an emergency.

## In practice
- Add sodium to bottles for long hot rides; salty foods at stops work too.
- Before a hot event: salted meals the day before; 500 ml with about 500–700 mg sodium 60–90 min before the start helps retain fluid.
- Cramps: most evidence links exercise cramps to muscle fatigue and unaccustomed intensity rather than electrolyte loss; sodium helps some heavy salty sweaters, but pacing and conditioning matter more.
- Potassium, magnesium and calcium losses in sweat are small; routine supplementation during rides is unnecessary.

## Limits
Sweat sodium testing varies by method and body site. Blood-pressure or kidney conditions change sodium advice and need a doctor.

## Coach guidance
Keep the message simple: sodium for long and hot, not for every ride. Flag confusion or vomiting during long events as a medical emergency.
