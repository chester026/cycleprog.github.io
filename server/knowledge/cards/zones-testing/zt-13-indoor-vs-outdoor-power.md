## Summary
Many riders produce less power indoors than outdoors at the same effort; some produce the same, a few more. The main causes are heat build-up without airflow, a fixed bike that prevents natural movement, different flywheel inertia, and motivation. A second, separate cause is measurement: a trainer and an on-bike power meter rarely agree exactly. Where a real gap exists, indoor sessions should use an indoor FTP.

## Key numbers
- Typical gap: 0–10% lower indoors; 3–5% is common among riders who train mostly outdoors.
- Without a fan, core temperature climbs quickly: sweat rates of 1–1.5 L/h and heart rate 10–15 beats higher at the same power are usual. Two strong fans largely remove the heat component.
- Trainer versus crank or pedal meter: 2–5% differences are normal (drivetrain loss 2–4% plus calibration).
- A time-trial comparison in trained cyclists found higher power outdoors than in a windless lab at the same perceived effort (about 208 W versus 163 W over 40 km; 12 riders).
- Adaptation: 2–4 weeks of regular indoor riding narrows the gap for most riders.

## In practice
- Use the same power meter indoors and outdoors where possible (power-match the trainer to the bike's meter).
- If a gap persists with good cooling, set an indoor FTP about 3–5% lower, or test indoors separately.
- Cooling checklist: large fan to the torso and face, a second from behind or the side, room under 18–20 °C if possible, 500–1000 ml/h fluid.
- ERG mode suits steady intervals; use resistance or slope mode for sprints, short intervals and for learning to pace.
- Standing periodically and a rocker plate reduce saddle pressure and stiffness.

## Limits
Evidence is limited to a few small studies; most of this is practice-based. Individual differences are large.

## Coach guidance
Keep indoor and outdoor bests separate when judging progress unless the same meter was used. Never read a lower indoor number as lost fitness.
