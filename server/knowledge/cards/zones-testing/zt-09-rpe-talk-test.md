## Summary
Rating of perceived exertion (RPE) integrates everything the body is experiencing into one number and tracks physiological thresholds surprisingly well. It is always available, has no lag and adjusts automatically for heat, altitude and fatigue. Skilled riders pace long efforts by feel and use devices as confirmation. Calibrating a rider's sense of effort against power and heart rate is a training goal in itself.

## Key numbers (CR-10 scale, during the effort)
| Intensity | RPE | Breathing and speech |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery | 1–2 | nose breathing, effortless talk |
| Endurance (LOW) | 2–3 | full sentences |
| Tempo | 4–5 | short sentences |
| Sweet spot / threshold | 6–7 | a few words; breathing deep and rhythmic |
| VO2max | 8–9 | single words; breathing near maximal |
| Anaerobic / sprint | 10 | cannot speak |

- Borg 6–20 scale equivalents: LT1 around 11–12, LT2 around 14–16.
- RPE rises steadily during a constant hard effort; a threshold interval that starts at 6 should end at 7–8, not start at 8.
- Isoeffort pacing of intervals (same final RPE in each rep) reproduces the intended stimulus well in trained athletes.

## In practice
- Teach anchors: "all day", "could talk for an hour", "comfortably hard", "counting minutes".
- In heat, at altitude or when tired, pace by RPE and heart rate and accept lower power.
- For long events, RPE in the first third should feel deliberately too easy.
- Mismatch tells a story: high RPE at normal power → fatigue, illness, low fuel, heat; low RPE at normal power → freshness or improvement.

## Limits
Beginners rate inconsistently. Music, competition, caffeine and mood lower perceived effort; mental fatigue raises it.

## Coach guidance
Ask for RPE routinely and use the rider's own words. For riders without power or heart-rate data, prescribe entirely by RPE and talk test.
