## Summary
Whether to save weight or drag depends on gradient and speed. Mass matters in proportion to how much of the power goes into lifting it, so it dominates only on sustained steep climbs. Aerodynamic savings apply everywhere there is speed, including valley roads and descents within a mountain event. For most amateur events with mixed terrain, the aerodynamic option is faster; a lighter bike wins on long steep climbs ridden slowly.

## Key numbers
- On a climb, 1 kg is about 1.2% of an 84 kg system: at 8% and 250 W it costs roughly 1% of climbing time: about 18–20 s on a 30-min climb, 35–40 s per hour of climbing.
- On the flat at 35 km/h, 1 kg costs about 0.4 W (through rolling resistance): practically nothing.
- A modest aerodynamic gain of 0.01 m² CdA saves about 5–6 W at 35 km/h but under 1 W at 18 km/h.
- Break-even gradient where 1 kg and a typical aero upgrade (10–15 W at 40 km/h) are equal: roughly 5–7% for amateurs at 3–4 W/kg; steeper for faster riders.
- Accelerations: extra mass costs energy only while accelerating and is largely returned when coasting; the "rotating weight counts double" effect is small in steady riding.
- Rider body mass: 1 kg of rider and 1 kg of bike are equivalent on a climb.

## In practice
- Hilly gran fondo with long valleys: aero position, fast tyres and a reasonable bike weight beat an ultralight setup.
- Hill-climb time trial or summit finish above 7–8%: lightest safe setup, low gears.
- Carrying less (one bottle fewer where refills exist, no unnecessary bags) is free weight saving.
- Do not chase rider weight loss for the sake of these numbers; lost power erases the gain (see `nut-19-weight-management`).

## Limits
Calculations assume steady riding without wind; gusty crosswinds make deep wheels harder to handle for light riders.

## Coach guidance
Answer "aero or light?" with the rider's actual course profile and speeds. Keep the discussion on equipment and carried load.
