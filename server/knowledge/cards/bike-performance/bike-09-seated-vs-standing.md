## Summary
Seated climbing is the economical default: the saddle supports body weight and the upper body stays quiet. Standing lets the rider use body weight and more muscle mass, which helps on steep pitches, in accelerations and at very high power, at the price of a higher heart rate and more aerodynamic drag. In trained cyclists the difference in oxygen cost at moderate gradients is small, so short standing periods are a useful way to change muscle loading without real penalty.

## Key numbers
- At moderate gradients (about 5%) and submaximal power, trained cyclists showed similar oxygen cost seated and standing, with heart rate about 5–8 beats higher when standing.
- At very high intensities (above roughly 90–95% of maximal aerobic power) and steep gradients, standing allowed longer time to exhaustion in one study.
- Gradient guide: under about 6–7% seated is generally better; above about 10% standing becomes competitive, especially for lighter riders.
- Cadence usually drops 5–15 rpm when standing; shift up one or two gears to keep speed.
- Typical use by good climbers: mostly seated, standing 10–30 s every few minutes and on steep ramps or hairpins.
- Aerodynamic cost of standing matters above about 20 km/h.

## In practice
- Stand to accelerate, to crest steep pitches, to relieve the back and saddle, and to keep momentum through hairpins.
- Technique: shift up before rising, keep the bike moving forward (do not let it drop back in a group), hips over the bottom bracket, light sway of the bike, hands on the hoods.
- Heavier riders pay more for standing; lighter riders often climb naturally out of the saddle.
- Practise both: include standing intervals of 30–60 s within climbing efforts.
- Late in long events, alternating positions delays local muscle fatigue.

## Limits
Studies are small and use short efforts; individual preference and body mass change the picture.

## Coach guidance
Do not prescribe a ratio. Suggest brief standing periods to riders who report back or saddle discomfort on long climbs.
