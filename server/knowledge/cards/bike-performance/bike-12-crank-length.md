## Summary
Within the range sold for road bikes, crank length has very little effect on the power a rider can produce or on efficiency: riders adapt cadence to keep pedal speed similar. Crank length is therefore a fit decision. Shorter cranks open the hip angle at the top of the pedal stroke, which helps riders with limited hip mobility, makes low aerodynamic positions easier to hold and can ease knee discomfort. That is the reason for the current move toward shorter cranks among professionals.

## Key numbers
- Maximal power differed by under about 4% across crank lengths from 145 to 195 mm in a controlled study; only the extremes were measurably worse.
- Submaximal efficiency: no meaningful difference between about 150 and 190 mm at matched pedal speed.
- Standard sizes: 165, 170, 172.5, 175 mm; increasingly 160–165 mm for time trial, triathlon and smaller riders.
- Shortening by 5 mm opens the hip angle at top dead centre by roughly 2–3° and reduces peak knee flexion by a similar amount.
- When shortening cranks: raise the saddle by the same amount to keep leg extension; expect cadence to rise by a few rpm; gearing feels slightly harder (about 3% per 5 mm), so one easier gear may be needed.
- Rough proportional guide: about 20–21% of inseam, with wide tolerance.

## In practice
- Consider shorter cranks for: tight hips in the aero position, knee pain at the top of the stroke, thighs hitting the torso, small riders on stock 170–172.5 mm cranks, criterium riders wanting pedal clearance.
- Long cranks offer no leverage advantage that gearing does not already provide.
- Allow 2–3 weeks to adapt before judging.
- Check power-meter compatibility and update the crank-length setting on pedal-based meters, or power readings will be wrong.

## Limits
Evidence covers short lab tests; long-term comfort and injury outcomes are based on fitting practice.

## Coach guidance
Treat as a comfort and position question, not a power upgrade. Suggest a professional fit before buying parts.
