## Summary
A bike fit sets the three contact points, saddle, handlebars and pedals, so that the rider can produce power comfortably for the duration of their events. Saddle height has the best evidence: too low costs efficiency and loads the front of the knee, too high causes rocking hips and strain at the back of the knee. Beyond that, fit is individual: flexibility, proportions, injury history and goals decide, and comfort that lasts is the main criterion.

## Key numbers
- Saddle height: knee angle of about 25–35° from full extension at the bottom of the stroke (measured statically), or 30–40° measured dynamically. Starting estimates: inseam × 0.883 (centre of bottom bracket to top of saddle), or heel on the pedal with a straight leg.
- Signs of too high: hips rocking, toes pointing down, pain behind the knee, saddle discomfort. Too low: pain at the front of the knee, cramped feeling, heavy quads.
- Adjust in steps of 2–3 mm; allow 1–2 weeks to adapt before judging.
- Saddle setback: knee roughly over the pedal axle with the crank forward is a starting convention, not a rule.
- Saddle tilt: level to 1–3° nose down.
- Cleats: ball of the foot over or slightly ahead of the pedal axle; rotation set so the foot sits in its natural angle; some float protects knees.
- Reach and drop: a neutral spine, slight bend in the elbows, no numb hands or neck strain after 2+ h.

## In practice
- Change one thing at a time and record measurements.
- Common symptom map: numb hands → too much weight forward or bars too low; lower-back pain → reach or drop too large, or weak trunk; saddle numbness → tilt, saddle shape or height.
- A professional fit is worth it for persistent discomfort, after injury, for a new bike or a time-trial position.
- Re-check after changing shoes, pedals, saddle or cranks.

## Limits
Formulas give starting points only; evidence for many fit rules beyond saddle height is weak.

## Coach guidance
Persistent pain or numbness is a reason to recommend a professional fit or a physiotherapist, not to train through. Do not diagnose injuries.
