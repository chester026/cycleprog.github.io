## Summary
Descending and cornering are skills, and they are worth both time and safety. A confident descender gains minutes in a mountain event and arrives at the bottom less tense and less tired. The fundamentals are few: look far ahead, brake before the turn, choose a late-apex line within one's own lane, keep the body relaxed and the outside pedal weighted. Speed comes from smoothness, never from risk.

## Key principles
- Vision: look through the corner to the exit; the bike follows the eyes.
- Braking: do most of it in a straight line before turning; the front brake provides most of the stopping power; use both, progressively. Release gradually as the bike leans.
- Line: enter wide, turn in late, apex late, exit wide, staying entirely in one's own lane on open roads.
- Body: hands in the drops for control and braking leverage, elbows bent, outside pedal down with weight on it, inside knee relaxed, hips back on steep sections.
- Wet roads: brake earlier and gentler, lean less, avoid paint, metal covers and leaves; stopping distances roughly double.
- Long descents: alternate firm braking with releasing rather than dragging the brakes; rim brakes on carbon rims and overheated discs fade.
- Position on straights: a low tuck on the hoods or drops is nearly as fast as extreme positions and far safer; sitting on the top tube is banned in competition and unstable.
- Tyres: correct pressure and good tread condition matter more here than anywhere.

## In practice
- Practise on a familiar, quiet descent: repeat single corners, increasing entry speed only slightly.
- Follow a skilled rider's line at a safe distance.
- Keep pedalling out of corners to regain speed rather than carrying risk into them.
- Stay warm: put on a gilet or jacket at the top; cold hands brake poorly.
- If fear spikes: slow down, sit up, breathe out; tension makes the bike handle worse.

## Limits
Road conditions, traffic and visibility set the limit; no technique compensates for the unknown beyond a blind bend.

## Coach guidance
Treat descending as a skill to practise, never as something to push. Safety advice always takes priority over time gains.
