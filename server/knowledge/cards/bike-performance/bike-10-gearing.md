## Summary
Gearing decides whether a rider can hold a sensible cadence at their sustainable power on the steepest part of a climb. Too large a lowest gear forces low cadence and high pedal force, which raises muscular fatigue and pushes riders above their planned power just to keep moving. Choosing the lowest gear from the event's steepest sustained gradient and the rider's W/kg is simple arithmetic and one of the cheapest ways to climb better.

## Key numbers
- Speed on a climb ≈ power / (mass × g × gradient), minus small losses. Example: 84 kg system, 200 W, 9%: about 9.2 km/h.
- Cadence at that speed (700c wheel, about 2.1 m circumference): 34×28 → 60 rpm; 34×32 → 69 rpm; 34×34 → 73 rpm; 30×34 (sub-compact) → 83 rpm.
- Target: at least 70 rpm, ideally 75–85 rpm, at planned power on the steepest sustained section.
- Rule of thumb for riders around 2.5–3.5 W/kg on climbs steeper than 8%: a 1:1 lowest gear (34×34) or lower.
- Stronger riders (4+ W/kg) manage 36×30 or 34×30 on most road climbs.
- Each additional lower gear costs a few tens of grams and nothing in speed elsewhere.
- Gravel and loaded touring: lowest gear under 1:1 (e.g. 40×44, 30×36).

## In practice
- Check derailleur capacity (largest sprocket and total chain wrap) before fitting a bigger cassette; a longer chain may be needed.
- For events with long descents or fast flats, keep an adequate top gear; spinning out above about 60 km/h matters little.
- Test the gearing on the steepest local climb at planned power before the event.
- Late in long events, cadence at a given gradient falls as power fades: choose gears for the tired version of the rider.
- Electronic or mechanical, shift early before the gradient bites.

## Limits
Calculations ignore wind and surface; steep sections shorter than about 1–2 min can be ridden out of the saddle in a bigger gear.

## Coach guidance
When a rider's climbing cadence in files sits under about 65 rpm for long periods, raise gearing as a practical fix before training solutions.
