## Summary
There is no single best cadence. The cadence that minimises oxygen cost is lower than the one experienced cyclists choose freely; riders trade a little metabolic efficiency for lower force per pedal stroke, which reduces muscular fatigue and keeps the ability to accelerate. Preferred cadence rises with power output and falls on climbs. In trained riders, the self-selected cadence is usually close to the best compromise.

## Key numbers
- Metabolically cheapest cadence: about 60–80 rpm at moderate power; it shifts upward as power rises.
- Freely chosen by trained cyclists: 85–100 rpm on the flat; 70–85 rpm on climbs; 95–110 rpm in time trials and track pursuit; sprint 110–130 rpm.
- Recreational riders often choose 70–85 rpm.
- At the same power, 60 rpm requires about 50% more pedal force than 90 rpm.
- Very low cadence (under about 60 rpm) at high power increases muscular strain and knee load; very high cadence at low power wastes energy moving the legs.
- Cadence typically drops 5–10 rpm with fatigue at a given power.

## In practice
- Let riders use what feels natural within about 75–100 rpm on the flat; intervene only at the extremes.
- On climbs, choose gearing that allows at least 70 rpm at target power on the steepest sustained sections.
- Higher cadence before and during accelerations makes surges cheaper; lower cadence can spare the cardiovascular system on long steady climbs.
- For long events, slightly higher cadence early spares the muscles for later.
- A marked drop in cadence during intervals is a useful fatigue signal.
- Being able to ride comfortably across a wide range is more valuable than hitting one number.

## Limits
Individual optima differ with fibre type, limb length and training history; forcing a cadence change in experienced riders rarely improves performance.

## Coach guidance
Do not prescribe a universal cadence. Comment only when cadence is very low on climbs (gearing issue) or falls sharply within a session.
