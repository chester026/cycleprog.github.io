## Summary
Tyres are the cheapest meaningful speed gain on a bike. Rolling resistance differs severalfold between tyre models, and it depends on casing, compound, inner tube, pressure and surface. On real roads, pumping tyres as hard as possible is slower: beyond a certain pressure the bike and rider are shaken by surface roughness and energy is lost in the body. Wider tyres at lower pressure roll as fast or faster on imperfect surfaces and add comfort and grip.

## Key numbers
- Rolling resistance coefficient (Crr): fast road tyres on smooth asphalt 0.0025–0.004; training tyres 0.005–0.007; rough tarmac adds 20–50%; gravel 0.008–0.020.
- Power: at 35 km/h for an 84 kg system, each 0.001 of Crr costs about 8 W. Fast versus slow tyres: commonly 10–20 W per pair.
- Inner tubes: latex or TPU instead of butyl saves about 2–5 W per pair; a good tubeless setup is similar.
- Starting pressures, road, 70–80 kg rider: 25 mm 5.5–6.5 bar (80–95 psi); 28 mm 4.5–5.5 bar (65–80 psi); 30–32 mm 3.8–4.8 bar (55–70 psi). Lower by 0.3–0.5 bar for wet or rough roads, raise slightly for heavier riders.
- Gravel 40 mm: 2.0–3.0 bar (30–45 psi) depending on surface and rider mass.
- Front can run about 0.2–0.4 bar lower than rear.

## In practice
- Choose the widest fast tyre that fits the frame and rim; match width to rim for aerodynamics if that matters.
- Check pressure before every ride; tyres lose pressure over days (latex within hours).
- Worn or old tyres roll slower and puncture more; replace before key events, a week ahead.
- Puncture protection adds rolling resistance; pick by the event's roads.
- Never exceed rim or tyre maximum pressure, especially with hookless rims.

## Limits
Lab drum tests overstate the benefit of high pressure; optimal pressure on a given road is found by feel or testing. Independent test data vary with protocol.

## Coach guidance
For any rider asking about speed upgrades, check tyres and pressure first.
