## Summary
A clean, well-lubricated chain drive transmits about 97–98% of the rider's power to the rear wheel. Dirt, wear, poor lubricant and extreme chain angles can cost several more percent. The gains from attention to the drivetrain are small in absolute terms but cheap, and a neglected drivetrain also shifts badly and wears expensive parts quickly.

## Key numbers
- Efficiency: about 96–98% for a clean chain at normal power; drops to 92–95% when dirty or dry.
- At 250 W: clean versus dirty chain is a difference of about 5–10 W.
- Lubricant choice: good wax or top drip lubricants versus poor ones: about 2–5 W.
- Chain line: big-big or small-small combinations cost about 1–3 W versus a straight line.
- Larger chainring and sprockets for the same ratio (e.g. 52×20 rather than 39×15) reduce friction slightly, about 1–2 W.
- Oversized pulley wheels: about 1–2 W at best.
- Chain wear: replace at about 0.5% elongation (11–12-speed) to protect cassette and chainrings; typical life 3000–6000 km depending on conditions and care.
- Efficiency improves at higher power and lower cadence for a given power (higher chain tension).

## In practice
- Clean and re-lubricate after wet or dusty rides and every 200–400 km otherwise; wipe off excess lubricant.
- Hot-melt or drip wax keeps the drivetrain clean and extends component life; it needs a fully degreased chain first.
- Before a key event: clean drivetrain, checked shifting, fresh cables or charged batteries.
- Bottom-bracket and hub bearings in good condition matter less than the chain; ceramic bearings give very small gains.

## Limits
Published efficiency tests use specific loads and conditions; marketing figures for individual products are often optimistic.

## Coach guidance
Mention maintenance when a rider asks about marginal gains or reports shifting or noise problems. Keep expectations modest.
