## Summary
Travel affects performance in two ways. Travel fatigue comes from any long journey: sitting, dehydration, disrupted meals and sleep; it clears in a day or two. Jet lag comes from crossing time zones: the body clock is out of step with local time, which disturbs sleep, alertness, digestion and performance until it re-synchronises. Light exposure at the right times is the main tool for shifting the clock.

## Key numbers
- Jet lag becomes noticeable from about 3 time zones.
- Adaptation rate: roughly 1 day per time zone eastward, 1 day per 1.5 zones westward (westward is easier).
- Arrive early: ideally 1 day per time zone crossed before an important event; at minimum 2–3 days for 3–6 zones.
- Performance and reaction time follow the body clock: hard efforts feel worst in the body's "night".
- Light: after eastward travel seek morning light (and avoid late-evening light) at destination once partly adapted; after westward travel seek evening light. For very large shifts the timing reverses in the first days; a jet-lag calculator helps.
- Melatonin 0.5–5 mg at destination bedtime can aid eastward adaptation; check local regulations and medical suitability.
- Short-haul or same-zone travel: allow the rest of the day as easy.

## In practice
- Before departure: shift sleep 30–60 min per day toward destination time for 2–3 days if practical; arrive rested.
- In transit: drink regularly, walk every hour or two, eat on destination schedule, limit alcohol.
- On arrival: easy 30–60 min spin, daylight, local meal times; avoid long daytime naps (20–30 min maximum).
- Training in the first 2–3 days: easy only; first quality session when sleep is roughly normal.
- Pack ride nutrition and key equipment in hand luggage.

## Limits
Individual differences (chronotype, age) are large; evidence for many popular remedies is weak.

## Coach guidance
Build travel days into the plan as recovery days and move key sessions accordingly. Medication and melatonin questions go to a doctor or pharmacist.
