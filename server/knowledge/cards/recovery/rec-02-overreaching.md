## Summary
Training hard enough to cause a temporary drop in performance is called overreaching. If performance rebounds above the previous level after days to about two weeks of recovery, it was functional. If underperformance, fatigue and mood disturbance last weeks to months, it is non-functional, a setback with no benefit. The difference is recognised only in hindsight, by how long recovery takes. Trial evidence suggests that deliberately pushing athletes into overreaching yields no more, and possibly less, than a hard overload that stops short of it.

## Key numbers
- Functional overreaching: performance restored or improved within about 1–2 weeks.
- Non-functional overreaching: recovery takes several weeks to months.
- In trained cyclists, 2 weeks of intensified training (about doubling time at high intensity) reduced time-trial power by about 5% and maximal heart rate by about 7–10 beats, with worse mood; partly recovered after 2 weeks.
- In triathletes, those who became overreached during 3 weeks of overload gained less after the taper than those who coped with the same overload without overreaching.

## Early signs
Power drop in key sessions despite high effort; heart rate that will not rise; raised resting heart rate or suppressed HRV trend; poor sleep despite tiredness; irritability, low mood, loss of drive; heavy legs; more frequent colds; appetite changes.

## In practice
- Two or more signs for more than about 5–7 days: take 5–7 very easy days and reassess.
- Check the usual accomplices: low energy and carbohydrate intake, poor sleep, work or family stress, travel, heat.
- If normal performance does not return after 2 weeks of reduced load, extend the rest and consider medical review.

## Limits
No single marker identifies overreaching reliably; performance testing plus mood is the best available combination.

## Coach guidance
Name the pattern early and prescribe rest plainly. Reassure that a week off costs little; continuing costs months.
