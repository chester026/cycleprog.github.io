## Summary
Some tiredness is the expected result of training and no reason to change the plan; other tiredness means the planned session will do more harm than good. Telling them apart is a core skill. Subjective wellbeing, how the rider says they feel, has been shown in a systematic review to track training response at least as well as objective markers, so the rider's own report is the first input, checked against simple objective signs and against the first minutes of the ride.

## Key numbers and rules
- Normal training fatigue: heavy legs that improve during warm-up; normal mood and sleep; normal heart-rate response. → Train as planned.
- Warm-up test: 10–15 min easy, then 2–3 min at the session's target. If power is reachable at usual RPE and heart rate responds, continue. If RPE is 2+ points higher or heart rate stays 8–10+ beats low, switch to easy or stop.
- First-interval rule: if the first interval is more than about 5% under target at maximal effort, abandon the session and ride easy.
- Easy day instead of hard: two or more of: poor sleep 2+ nights, resting heart rate +5 beats, HRV trend suppressed, sore throat, unusual irritability, very high work or family stress.
- Full rest: fever, illness symptoms below the neck, dizziness, sleep under about 4–5 h.
- A postponed hard session moves by a day; it is not added on top of the next one.

## In practice
- Tired before LOW rides: usually fine to ride; shorten if it does not lift.
- Tired before HIGH sessions: quality matters; a mediocre interval session costs recovery without the benefit.
- Tiredness that is mental (work day, decisions) raises RPE but not physiological strain: an easy ride often helps; maximal sessions rarely go well.
- A pattern of frequent swaps means the weekly load or layout needs changing.

## Limits
Rules are heuristics; individual patterns differ and should be learned over time.

## Coach guidance
Back the rider's decision to rest. Offer the warm-up test when they are unsure, and praise good judgement when they stop.
