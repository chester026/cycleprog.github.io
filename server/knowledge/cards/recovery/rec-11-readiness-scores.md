## Summary
Daily readiness or recovery scores compress night-time heart rate, HRV, sleep and recent activity into one number. The underlying signals are meaningful; the composite scores are proprietary, unvalidated against performance and sometimes wrong in both directions. A systematic review found that athletes' own ratings of wellbeing reflect training response more consistently than common objective markers. The sensible position: a score is a second opinion, the rider's feeling and the warm-up are the first.

## Key numbers
- Typical agreement between a low score and a genuinely poor session is modest; many low-score days produce normal training.
- Night-to-night noise in HRV (10–20%) feeds directly into scores; trends over 3–7 days are more reliable than single mornings.
- Small trials of HRV-guided training show results equal to or better than fixed plans, using rolling averages and individual thresholds, not daily consumer scores.

## Decision matrix
| Score | Feeling | Action |
|---|---|---|
| High | Good | Train as planned |
| High | Bad | Trust the feeling: warm-up test, be ready to go easy |
| Low | Good | Start as planned; judge by warm-up and first interval |
| Low | Bad | Easy day or rest |
| Low for 3+ days | Any | Reduce load for a few days; check sleep, illness, stress, fuelling |

## In practice
- Expected low scores: the morning after hard or long sessions, alcohol, late meals, travel, heat, poor sleep, illness onset, menstrual-cycle phase for some women.
- A high score is not permission to add intensity beyond the plan.
- If checking the score causes anxiety or overrides how the rider feels, hide it for a few weeks.
- The components (resting heart rate up, temperature up, sleep short) explain more than the number.

## Limits
Algorithms differ between brands and change with updates; none publish validation against training outcomes.

## Coach guidance
Never cancel or add a session on a score alone. Combine it with the rider's report and say explicitly which signal is behind any suggestion.
