## Summary
Sleep is the most powerful recovery tool available and costs nothing. Restricted sleep raises perceived effort, impairs glycogen restoration, mood, immune function and decision-making, and reduces the ability to sustain hard efforts, while a single short night has surprisingly little effect on brief maximal performance. Extending sleep improves performance and wellbeing in athletes who are short of it. Needs are individual.

## Key numbers
- Need: 7–9 h for most adults; athletes in heavy training often do better with 8–10 h including naps.
- After one night of partial sleep loss: endurance time-trial performance typically falls a few percent, mainly through higher RPE; short maximal efforts are largely preserved.
- Several nights under about 6 h: clear decline in training quality, mood and illness resistance.
- Naps: 20–30 min (or about 90 min for a full cycle) before mid-afternoon improve alertness without harming night sleep.
- Caffeine half-life about 4–6 h: avoid within 6–8 h of bedtime.
- Alcohol fragments the second half of the night and suppresses HRV.
- Hard evening sessions can delay sleep onset by raising core temperature and arousal; finishing 2–3 h before bed helps.

## In practice
- Regular bed and wake times, a cool dark room (about 16–19 °C), no screens in the last 30–60 min, and a wind-down routine.
- Before an early event: bank sleep over the preceding week; the night two days before matters more than the last one.
- When sleep was poor: keep easy training, move HIGH sessions if RPE is clearly raised.
- Parents of young children and shift workers: plan load around realistic sleep, not ideal sleep.

## Limits
Wearable sleep staging is approximate. Worrying about sleep scores can itself harm sleep. Persistent insomnia, loud snoring with daytime sleepiness or breathing pauses need medical assessment.

## Coach guidance
Treat sleep as part of the training plan. Ask about it when performance dips, without lecturing riders whose circumstances limit it.
