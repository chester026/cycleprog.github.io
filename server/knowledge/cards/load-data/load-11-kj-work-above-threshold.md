## Summary
Mechanical work in kilojoules is the most direct output of a power meter: average power times time. It needs no FTP, so it is a robust volume measure and the basis for energy and fuelling estimates. On its own it says nothing about intensity; splitting it into work below and above threshold shows how costly a ride was. Field data from professionals show that work done above critical power impairs later performance more than the same amount of work below it.

## Key numbers
- kJ = average power (W) × seconds / 1000. 200 W for 3 h = 2160 kJ.
- Energy expended in kcal ≈ mechanical kJ (gross efficiency about 20–25% roughly cancels the 4.18 kJ/kcal conversion). Error about ±5–10%.
- Typical rides: 1 h easy 400–700 kJ; 3 h endurance 1500–2500 kJ; hard gran fondo 3000–5000 kJ; pro stage 3500–6000 kJ.
- Body-mass normalized work for fatigue comparisons: 20–50 kJ/kg covers a long ride to a hard pro race.
- In professional riders, work accumulated above CP reduced subsequent maximal power and CP progressively (reported drops of roughly 2%, 6% and 16% in CP after 2.5, 5.0 and 7.5 kJ/kg above CP), while matched work below CP had little effect.

## In practice
- Fuelling: carbohydrate needs scale with kJ/h and intensity; a 700 kJ/h ride burns roughly 100–150 g of carbohydrate per hour at MID intensity, less at LOW.
- Weekly kJ is a good volume metric across indoor and outdoor rides and is insensitive to FTP errors.
- Report "kJ above FTP" for races, group rides and interval days to explain fatigue that TSS understates.

## Limits
Efficiency varies between riders (about 19–25%), so calorie estimates are approximate. kJ ignores environmental and non-cycling stress.

## Coach guidance
Use kJ for energy and fuelling advice and for comparing ride size. When explaining fatigue, separate easy work from work above threshold.
