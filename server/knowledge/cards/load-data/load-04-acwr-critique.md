## Summary
The acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) divides the last week's load by the average of the last four. A "sweet spot" of 0.8–1.3 was promoted as protecting against injury, mostly from team-sport data. Methodological critiques have since shown that the ratio has no established causal link to injury, has statistical flaws, and can generate artefacts. It should not be used as a risk predictor or a prescription tool, in cycling or elsewhere.

## Key numbers
- Claimed safe zone 0.8–1.3 and danger zone above 1.5: not supported as causal thresholds.
- Substituting random or fixed values for the chronic load reproduced similar "associations", which undermines the theory behind the ratio.

## Why it fails
- The ratio turned an additive fitness–fatigue idea into a division without justification.
- Acute load is part of chronic load in the coupled version, creating built-in correlation.
- Ratios hide absolute load: 1.2 at very low load and 1.2 at very high load are not comparable.
- Studies were associations with many analytic choices, not tests of whether changing the ratio changes outcomes.

## In practice
- Sudden large jumps in load are still unwise; that is ordinary progressive-overload principle and does not need a ratio.
- Prefer absolute week-to-week change, the content of the load, and the rider's response.
- Several apps display ACWR-like "load focus" or "optimal range" bands; treat them as descriptive.

## Limits
The critique does not say load spikes are harmless, only that this metric cannot quantify the risk. Cycling-specific injury research on load is thin in any case.

## Coach guidance
Do not cite ACWR ranges as evidence. If a rider asks about an app's ratio, explain what it describes and why it is not a predictor, then return to load progression and how they feel.
