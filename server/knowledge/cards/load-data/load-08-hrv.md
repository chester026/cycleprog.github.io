## Summary
Resting heart-rate variability (HRV), usually reported as rMSSD, reflects parasympathetic activity and responds to training load, illness, alcohol, poor sleep and psychological stress. Single daily values are noisy; the rolling 7-day average compared with the rider's own normal range is what carries information. Small trials suggest that adjusting hard days according to HRV gives results at least as good as a fixed plan.

## Key numbers
- Day-to-day variation of rMSSD: commonly 10–20%. Absolute values differ hugely between people (20–150 ms) and decline with age; never compare riders.
- Useful signal: 7-day average outside the individual normal band (about ±0.5–1 SD of the last 30–60 days) for several days.
- HRV-guided trial in well-trained cyclists (17 riders, 8 weeks): the guided group improved peak power by about 5% and 40-min time-trial power by about 7%, the fixed-plan group did not improve significantly; the difference between the groups was not statistically significant (small study).

## In practice
- Measure the same way every day: overnight (ring/watch) or on waking, lying or sitting, 1–5 min.
- Suppressed 7-day average + higher resting HR + poor feeling: postpone HIGH work, ride easy.
- A single low morning with good feeling: train as planned.
- HRV rising above normal with falling resting HR during heavy load can be a sign of parasympathetic saturation or deep fatigue, not of super-fitness; check feeling and performance.
- Expect lower values after alcohol, late meals, travel, heat and in the days before illness.

## Limits
Devices compute HRV differently (night average vs short morning reading) and are not interchangeable. Evidence for HRV-guided training comes from small, short trials. HRV cannot say what kind of stress is present.

## Coach guidance
Use HRV as one input alongside feeling and recent load, and say so. Never override how a rider feels because a number is "green" or "red".
