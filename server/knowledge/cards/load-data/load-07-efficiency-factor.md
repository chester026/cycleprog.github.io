## Summary
Efficiency factor (EF) is Normalized Power divided by average heart rate for a ride or segment. Rising EF on comparable aerobic rides means more power for the same cardiovascular cost, a simple sign of improving aerobic fitness. Research on standardized submaximal tests supports the underlying idea: power at a fixed submaximal heart rate tracks performance changes in trained cyclists.

## Key numbers
- EF = NP (W) / average HR (bpm). Typical amateur values 1.2–1.9; the absolute number only has meaning relative to the same rider.
- Meaningful change: about 3–5% sustained across several comparable rides. Day-to-day variation of heart rate is 2–4 beats/min, i.e. about 2–3% of EF.
- Typical base-phase improvement over 8–12 weeks: 5–10% in riders with room to improve.

## In practice
- Compare only steady LOW or MID rides of similar duration, terrain and temperature, ideally the same route or an indoor session.
- Equivalent view that riders understand more easily: "at 140 bpm you now hold 185 W instead of 172 W".
- EF falling while load rises: accumulated fatigue, heat, illness onset or dehydration. EF rising while heart rate is unusually suppressed and RPE is high: deep fatigue rather than fitness.

## Limits
EF mixes fitness with everything else that moves heart rate. It says little about ability above threshold. Rides with many stops or surges distort it. Changes in HR sensor or position (indoor vs outdoor) break the series.

## Coach guidance
Show EF as a trend with at least 4–6 comparable rides, and translate it into watts at a familiar heart rate. Avoid judging a single ride's EF.
