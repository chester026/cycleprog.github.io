## Summary
Chronic Training Load (CTL, "fitness") is an exponentially weighted average of daily TSS over about 42 days; Acute Training Load (ATL, "fatigue") the same over about 7 days; Training Stress Balance (TSB, "form") is yesterday's CTL minus yesterday's ATL. The chart is a simplified descendant of the Banister impulse–response model. CTL measures how much load a rider has been absorbing, not how fit they are.

## Key numbers
- Time constants: 42 days (CTL), 7 days (ATL); conventions, not individual values.
- CTL orientation (practice; depends on FTP accuracy): 30–50 recreational 4–6 h/week; 50–80 committed amateur 7–12 h; 80–110 high-level amateur; 110–150+ professional.
- TSB orientation: −10 to −30 productive training; below −30 heavy overload, limit to planned blocks or camps; −10 to +5 neutral; +5 to +25 fresh, typical for race day; above +25 for long periods means detraining.
- Taper: CTL usually falls 5–10% while TSB rises into the positive range.

## Why it works
Each session produces a slow-decaying positive effect and a fast-decaying negative one. Performance readiness is high when accumulated work is high and recent work is low.

## In practice
- Use CTL trend to check that load is building (or being held) as planned; use TSB to time recovery weeks and tapers.
- Two riders with CTL 70 can differ completely: one from 12 h easy riding, one from 7 h mostly at threshold.
- After a break, ATL and CTL understate how hard a normal session will feel.

## Limits
The model predicts individual performance poorly in research; time constants vary between people; it knows nothing about the type of training or life stress. Chasing a CTL number encourages junk load.

## Coach guidance
Describe CTL as "training load you are carrying" rather than fitness. Never recommend adding load only to raise CTL. Confirm conclusions with performance markers (power at durations, heart rate at given power).
