## Summary
Cycling's focus on power-to-weight makes it a higher-risk sport for disordered eating, in men as well as women and at amateur as well as elite level. Disordered eating covers a spectrum from rigid dieting and guilt around food to clinical eating disorders, which are serious illnesses. People affected often look fit and train well for a time. Early, kind recognition and a route to professional help matter more than anything a coach can say about nutrition. This card is for recognition and referral only.

## Signs that warrant concern
- Preoccupation with weight, body shape or leanness; frequent weighing; distress at small changes.
- Rigid food rules, cutting out food groups, skipping meals, avoiding eating with others, anxiety when plans change.
- Training to "earn" or "burn off" food; inability to take rest days; riding when ill or injured.
- Eating very little on or after long rides despite knowing the guidance.
- Reports of losing control around food, or of compensating after eating.
- Rapid weight change; in women, missed periods; in anyone, fatigue, feeling cold, dizziness, recurrent injuries or illness, falling performance.
- Mood changes: irritability, withdrawal, low mood, secrecy about eating.

## How to respond
- Express concern for the person, not their weight or performance, in plain, non-judging words.
- Encourage speaking with a doctor, and where available a sports dietitian and a psychologist experienced with eating disorders; offer to help find appropriate support if asked.
- Keep training advice conservative: no load increases, no fasted or restrictive strategies.
- Urgent medical care for fainting, chest pain, very low heart rate with symptoms, or thoughts of self-harm.

## What not to do
Do not give calorie numbers, weight targets, body-fat goals, meal plans, fasting schedules or weight-loss rates. Do not praise weight loss, compare bodies, or offer theories about why the person eats as they do.

## Limits
Only qualified clinicians can assess and diagnose; signs overlap with other conditions.

## Coach guidance
Once restriction, purging, bingeing or compensating has been mentioned, give no numbers about food or body for the rest of the conversation, even if asked or told it is for someone else. Stay warm, stay with the person, and keep pointing to professional help.
