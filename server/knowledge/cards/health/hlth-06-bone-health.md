## Summary
Bone adapts to impact and heavy loading. Cycling provides neither: the saddle carries body weight and forces on the skeleton are low. Reviews find that road cyclists, especially those who have trained for years with high volume and no other sport, tend to have lower bone mineral density than runners or even inactive people, particularly at the spine and hip. Low energy availability and low body mass add to the risk. The consequences show up as fractures from crashes that are worse than expected, and later in life.

## Key numbers
- Studies in elite and long-term road cyclists report low bone density in a substantial share of riders, including young men.
- Bone mass peaks in the mid-20s to about 30; adolescence is the main window for building it.
- From about 40, bone is lost gradually; in women the loss accelerates for several years around menopause.
- Mountain biking and cyclocross load bone more than road cycling.
- Calcium needs: about 1000–1300 mg per day from food for most adults and adolescents; vitamin D adequacy matters (see `nut-18-iron-vitamin-d`).

## What supports bone
- Heavy strength training 2 times a week year-round.
- Regular impact: brief jumping or hopping sets, running, skipping, racket or ball sports, hiking with descents.
- Eating enough to cover training; adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D.
- Not smoking; moderate alcohol.

## Who should talk to a doctor about bone assessment
Riders with a stress fracture or a fracture from a minor fall; long-term high-volume cyclists with no impact or strength work; anyone with a history of low energy availability, eating disorder or missed periods; men with low libido and fatigue; women after menopause; long-term users of steroid medication; a family history of osteoporosis.

## Limits
Most data are cross-sectional; how much strength and impact work is needed to offset cycling specifically is not well defined.

## Coach guidance
Use bone health as a positive reason for strength and some impact work, without alarm. Recommend medical assessment for the risk groups; do not advise on supplements beyond food sources.
