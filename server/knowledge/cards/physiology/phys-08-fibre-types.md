## Summary
Human muscle contains slow type I fibres (fatigue-resistant, efficient, oxidative) and fast type II fibres (IIa: fast and fairly oxidative; IIx: fastest, most powerful, quickly fatigued). Fibres are recruited in order of size: low forces use type I only, higher forces add IIa and then IIx. Recruitment also expands with duration, as tired fibres are replaced. Training changes fibre properties substantially and fibre type proportions only a little.

## Key numbers
- Vastus lateralis, average adult: about 50% type I. Elite endurance cyclists: 60–80%; elite sprinters: 30–40%.
- Recruitment by intensity (approximate): below LT1 mostly type I; LT1–LT2 type I plus growing share of IIa; above LT2 substantial IIa and IIx; sprints all.
- Proportions are largely inherited; long-term endurance training shifts IIx toward IIa and may raise type I share by a few percentage points over years.
- Type I fibres produce the same work with less oxygen: riders with more type I have higher gross efficiency.
- Peak power per unit of muscle is roughly 3–5 times higher in type II than type I fibres.

## In practice
- Endurance training makes type IIa fibres far more oxidative; this trained-fibre effect matters more than changing types.
- Long rides train fibres that short rides never reach, because recruitment widens as glycogen falls in the first-recruited fibres.
- Hard intervals and sprints are the only way to train the highest-threshold fibres; low-cadence work increases force per stroke but still well below maximal.
- Fibre profile helps explain rider type (sprinter vs time-trialist) and individual response to training.

## Limits
Fibre typing requires biopsy; indirect estimates from power profiles or sensors are rough. Many fibres are hybrids, and classification simplifies a continuum.

## Coach guidance
Use fibre-type reasoning to explain why variety of intensity and duration matters. Do not tell a rider their fibre type from power data as fact.
