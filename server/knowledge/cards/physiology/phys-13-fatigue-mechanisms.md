## Summary
Fatigue is a reduction in the ability to produce power, and it has several causes that dominate at different intensities and durations. Peripheral fatigue arises in the muscle itself; central fatigue is a reduced drive from the nervous system; and the decision to slow down is made through perceived effort, which integrates both with motivation. No single factor explains all fatigue, and models that centre on the muscle and models that centre on the brain remain in debate.

## Key numbers
- Sprints and efforts above critical power (seconds to about 20 min): phosphocreatine depletion, accumulation of inorganic phosphate, hydrogen ions and extracellular potassium impair contraction; recovery largely within minutes.
- Threshold-type efforts (20–60 min): combination of metabolite build-up, rising temperature and effort perception.
- Long rides (2 h+): glycogen depletion (performance falls sharply when muscle glycogen is low), dehydration above about 2–3% body mass in heat, core temperature approaching about 39.5–40 °C, muscle damage, central fatigue.
- Hypoglycaemia (blood glucose under about 3.5 mmol/L) causes weakness, dizziness and poor concentration.
- After exhaustive cycling, riders can still produce a brief sprint far above the power they just "could not" hold, showing that task failure is not complete muscle failure.

## Competing views
- Peripheral/critical-threshold view: feedback from fatiguing muscle limits drive to protect it.
- Psychobiological model: exercise stops when effort feels maximal or exceeds what the person is willing to give; mental fatigue and motivation shift the limit.
- Central-governor model: the brain regulates output in anticipation to protect homeostasis.
These overlap more than their proponents suggest; all agree perception of effort is the final common path.

## In practice
- Match the countermeasure to the cause: carbohydrate for long rides, cooling for heat, pacing for efforts above CP, sleep and reduced mental load for effort perception.
- Residual fatigue the next day is usually glycogen, muscle damage or sleep, not lactate.

## Limits
Mechanisms are hard to isolate in whole-body exercise; much evidence comes from isolated muscle or small lab studies.

## Coach guidance
When a rider fades, look for the most likely cause from duration, intensity, fuelling and conditions before attributing it to fitness.
