## Summary
When power rises, oxygen uptake does not jump to the new requirement; it climbs exponentially over a couple of minutes. The shortfall, the oxygen deficit, is covered by phosphocreatine and glycolysis. Faster kinetics mean a smaller deficit and less early fatigue. Above the first threshold a second, slow rise appears: oxygen cost creeps upward at constant power as efficiency falls. Above critical power this slow component does not stop until VO2max is reached.

## Key numbers
- Time constant of the primary phase: about 20–30 s in trained endurance athletes, 35–50 s in untrained adults, longer with age and inactivity. Steady state is reached after about 4 time constants (1.5–3 min).
- Slow component: absent below LT1; adds roughly 5–15% to oxygen cost over 10–20 min in the heavy domain; in the severe domain drives VO2 to maximum.
- Prior hard exercise (priming) 6–15 min earlier speeds the overall response and can extend time to exhaustion by 10–30% in lab studies.
- Endurance training speeds kinetics within weeks.

## Why it matters
- Every surge has an oxygen-deficit cost paid from W′; riders with fast kinetics pay less for the same acceleration.
- The slow component is closely tied to progressive recruitment of less efficient fibres and to fatigue developing in the working muscle.
- It explains why heart rate and breathing keep climbing in a steady hard effort and why "just above threshold" cannot be held.

## In practice
- Warm up before short hard efforts and races so the first effort starts with elevated oxygen uptake.
- In intervals, the first 1–2 min of each rep are spent reaching high VO2; hard starts and short recoveries increase time near maximum.
- Even pacing or a slightly fast start suits efforts of 2–5 min; a much too fast start drains W′ before the aerobic system catches up.

## Limits
Individual kinetics need lab measurement. Priming benefits depend on intensity and recovery time; too hard or too close harms performance.

## Coach guidance
Use to justify warm-ups and pacing advice in plain terms: "your aerobic engine needs about two minutes to get up to speed".
