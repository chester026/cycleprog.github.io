## Summary
Two thresholds divide exercise into three intensity domains. Below the first (LT1, VT1, "aerobic threshold") lactate stays at resting level and exercise is sustainable for hours: the moderate domain. Between the two, lactate is elevated but stabilises: the heavy domain. Above the second (LT2, VT2, MLSS, critical power, "anaerobic threshold") lactate and oxygen uptake keep rising until exhaustion: the severe domain. The many names describe the same two transitions measured in different ways; the methods do not give identical power values.

## Key numbers
- LT1: first rise of lactate above baseline, around 1.5–2.5 mmol/L; about 55–75% of VO2max; higher in well-trained athletes.
- LT2 / maximal steady state: typically 75–90% of VO2max in trained people; lactate at steady state anywhere from about 2.5 to 7 mmol/L between individuals.
- MLSS definition: highest constant power at which lactate rises less than 1 mmol/L between minutes 10 and 30.
- Sustainable time: below LT1 several hours (limited by fuel and fatigue); at the second threshold about 30–60 min.
- Training moves both thresholds to higher power and to a higher fraction of VO2max; threshold power can keep improving after VO2max has plateaued.

## In practice
- Power at the second threshold is the best single predictor of performance in efforts from 20 min to several hours.
- LT1 determines how hard "easy" can be and correlates with long-event performance and durability.
- Which estimate of the second threshold to use (FTP, CP, MLSS, 4 mmol/L) matters less than using one consistently.

## Limits
Experts dispute which method best represents the maximal metabolic steady state: critical power generally lies a little above MLSS, and both carry measurement error. Thresholds are not fixed: they drop with fatigue, heat and glycogen depletion.

## Coach guidance
Explain thresholds as two "gear changes" in how the body copes, then name the rider's own values in watts and heart rate.
