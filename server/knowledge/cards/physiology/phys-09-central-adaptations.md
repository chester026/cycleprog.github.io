## Summary
Central adaptations raise the amount of oxygen delivered to muscle. Endurance training expands blood volume, first plasma within days and then red-cell mass over weeks to months, and enlarges and strengthens the heart so that each beat ejects more blood. Maximal heart rate does not rise. These changes are the main reason VO2max improves, and they are also the first to reverse when training stops.

## Key numbers
- Plasma volume: +5–10% within the first 1–2 weeks of training or heat exposure; up to 15–20% in trained athletes.
- Haemoglobin mass: rises slowly, a few percent over months; elite endurance athletes have 30–50% more per kg than untrained people.
- Stroke volume at maximum: about 100–120 mL untrained, 150–200+ mL in elite endurance athletes.
- Resting heart rate: falls by 5–15+ beats/min with training; values of 40–50 are common in trained riders.
- Heart rate at a fixed submaximal power: drops by 10–20 beats over the first months of training.
- Maximal heart rate: unchanged or 3–5 beats lower.
- Each 1 g/kg increase in haemoglobin mass corresponds to roughly 4 mL/kg/min of VO2max.

## What drives them
Sustained high cardiac output (volume of training), repeated near-maximal filling of the heart (HIGH intervals), and stimuli that expand plasma: heat, dehydration–rehydration cycles, and training itself. Altitude and adequate iron support red-cell mass.

## In practice
- Early training gains (lower heart rate at the same power within 2–3 weeks) are mostly plasma volume.
- Heat training can add central benefit even in cool-weather riders.
- Low iron stores blunt the red-cell response; unexplained fatigue with stagnation is a reason for a medical check, not for self-supplementation.

## Limits
Individual responses vary widely. Very large hearts and low resting heart rates are normal in athletes but palpitations, fainting or chest symptoms always need a doctor.

## Coach guidance
Explain a falling heart rate at given power as real early progress. Refer any cardiac symptom or suspected deficiency to medical care.
