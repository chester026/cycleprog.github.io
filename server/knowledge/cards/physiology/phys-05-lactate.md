## Summary
Lactate is produced continuously, even at rest, as the end product of glycolysis. It is a major fuel: it moves from the fibres that produce it to neighbouring fibres, the heart, brain and liver, where it is oxidised or rebuilt into glucose. Blood lactate concentration reflects the balance between production and clearance. It does not cause muscle soreness and is not itself the cause of the "burn"; it is a useful marker because it rises alongside the metabolic changes that do limit performance.

## Key numbers
- Rest: 0.8–1.5 mmol/L. Easy riding: near resting. Second threshold: about 2.5–7 mmol/L individually. After maximal 1–4 min efforts: 12–20+ mmol/L.
- During exercise about 70–80% of lactate is oxidised as fuel; much of the remainder goes to glucose production in the liver.
- Clearance half-time after hard efforts: about 15–25 min at rest, faster with easy pedalling at 30–50% of VO2max.
- Blood lactate returns to baseline within 30–90 min; soreness appearing 24–48 h later has other causes (muscle micro-damage).
- Trained muscle has more transport proteins (MCT1/MCT4) and mitochondria, so it clears more at any given power.

## In practice
- A rightward shift of the lactate curve means better clearance and lower production at a given power, the essence of endurance adaptation.
- Easy spinning after hard efforts speeds lactate removal but this has little bearing on next-day recovery.
- High lactate with high power is a sign of strong glycolytic capacity, valuable for short events; the goal is not to minimise lactate everywhere.

## Limits
Whether acidity limits performance is debated: hydrogen ions, phosphate and potassium interact, and their relative roles differ with effort duration. Single lactate readings vary with diet, glycogen status and sampling technique.

## Coach guidance
Gently correct "lactic acid" myths when they affect decisions (e.g. flushing rides, soreness explanations). Otherwise keep the rider's familiar vocabulary.
