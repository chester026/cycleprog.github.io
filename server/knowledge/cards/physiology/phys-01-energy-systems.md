## Summary
Muscle runs on ATP, resynthesised by three overlapping systems: phosphocreatine (immediate, tiny store), glycolysis producing lactate (fast, limited), and oxidative metabolism of carbohydrate and fat (slower, practically unlimited). All three work from the first second; what changes with duration is their share. Aerobic metabolism dominates much earlier than riders tend to think, which is why aerobic fitness supports nearly every cycling effort longer than a sprint.

## Key numbers
- Phosphocreatine: dominant for about 5–10 s; largely depleted after 10–30 s all-out; half restored in about 30 s, near-full in 3–5 min.
- Glycolysis: peaks around 10–30 s; major contributor to efforts of 30 s–2 min.
- Aerobic share of maximal efforts: about 5–10% at 6 s; 30–45% at 30 s; about 50% at 75 s; 65–70% at 2 min; 80–85% at 4 min; 95%+ at 20 min; 98–99% at 1 h.
- ATP yield per glucose: 2–3 anaerobically, about 30 aerobically.
- Maximal rate of ATP supply: phosphocreatine > glycolysis > carbohydrate oxidation > fat oxidation (fat about half the rate of carbohydrate).

## In practice
- A 4-min climb or pursuit is mainly an aerobic event; VO2max and threshold work help it more than sprint work does.
- Repeated sprints depend on phosphocreatine recovery, which is an aerobic process: fitter riders recover faster between attacks.
- The order of power-supply rates explains why intensity forces a shift from fat to carbohydrate.

## Limits
Shares are estimates from oxygen-deficit methods with wide individual variation; sprinters derive more from anaerobic sources at every duration.

## Coach guidance
Use to correct the idea that "anaerobic" efforts start at threshold: above threshold the aerobic system is still doing most of the work, it just cannot cover all of it.
