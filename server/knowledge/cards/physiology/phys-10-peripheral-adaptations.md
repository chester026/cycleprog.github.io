## Summary
Peripheral adaptations change what the muscle does with delivered oxygen. Training increases the number and quality of mitochondria, the enzymes of fat and carbohydrate oxidation, the capillaries around each fibre, and the transporters for glucose, fatty acids and lactate. The result is less lactate and less glycogen use at any given power: the basis of higher thresholds and better endurance. These adaptations respond to both volume and intensity.

## Key numbers
- Mitochondrial content (citrate synthase activity): +20–40% after 6–12 weeks in previously untrained people; elite endurance athletes have roughly 2–3 times untrained levels.
- Capillaries per fibre: +10–30% over months of training.
- Mitochondrial enzymes turn over fast: half-time of roughly 1–2 weeks, so gains appear within 2 weeks and are lost quickly without stimulus.
- Reviews indicate training volume relates most closely to mitochondrial content, and intensity to mitochondrial respiratory function.
- Very low intensities (below roughly 60% of maximal work rate) produce little mitochondrial gain in untrained people.

## Signals
Energy turnover (AMPK), calcium cycling (CaMK), oxidative stress and low glycogen converge on regulators such as PGC-1α that switch on mitochondrial biogenesis. Longer and harder sessions both raise the signal; intensity does so more per minute, duration through accumulated time.

## In practice
- Thresholds improve mainly through these adaptations, which is why they keep rising for years.
- A mix of high volume and some high intensity covers both content and function.
- Consistency matters because of fast turnover: three weeks off erases a large part of recent mitochondrial gains.

## Limits
Most molecular data come from short studies in untrained or moderately trained people. Signalling responses do not always predict long-term adaptation or performance.

## Coach guidance
Use to explain why regular training beats sporadic big efforts, and why both long rides and hard sessions have a place.
