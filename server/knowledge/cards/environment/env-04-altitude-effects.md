## Summary
With increasing altitude the air holds less oxygen per breath, so maximal aerobic power and every threshold derived from it fall. The decline starts lower than many riders expect and is roughly linear. Thinner air also means less aerodynamic drag, which helps on fast flat sections and descents but not on climbs. In the first days, heart rate and breathing are elevated, sleep is poorer and dehydration is easier. Above about 2500 m, altitude illness becomes a real possibility.

## Key numbers
- VO2max falls about 6–7% per 1000 m, beginning from around 300–600 m in trained athletes; highly trained athletes are affected more.
- Sustainable (threshold) power when unacclimatised: about −3–5% at 1500 m, −6–9% at 2000 m, −10–14% at 2500 m, −14–18% at 3000 m. After 1–2 weeks of acclimatisation roughly a third to half of the loss is recovered.
- Short sprints are not impaired; recovery between efforts is slower.
- Air density: about 15% lower at 1500 m, 20% at 2000 m: higher speed for the same power on the flat.
- Acute responses: resting and submaximal heart rate +10–20%, higher ventilation, increased fluid loss through breathing and urine, reduced appetite, disturbed sleep for several nights.
- Acute mountain sickness (headache plus nausea, dizziness, fatigue, poor sleep): affects a substantial minority above 2500 m, more with rapid ascent. Worsening headache, breathlessness at rest, confusion or loss of coordination require immediate descent and medical help.

## In practice
- For events at altitude: arrive either 1–2 weeks early to acclimatise, or as late as practical (within about 24 h) to avoid the worst days 2–5.
- Lower power targets by the percentages above; pace by heart rate and breathing.
- Drink more, eat carbohydrate-rich meals, limit alcohol, expect poorer sleep.
- Sun exposure and cold are both more intense: sunscreen and layers.
- Zones from sea level do not apply; easy rides must be easier.

## Limits
Individual responses vary widely and cannot be predicted from fitness. People with heart or lung disease, sickle-cell trait or pregnancy need medical advice before high-altitude travel.

## Coach guidance
Adjust expectations and targets explicitly for altitude and make the illness warning signs clear.
