## Summary
Repeated exposure to heat stress over one to two weeks produces a reliable set of adaptations: expanded plasma volume, lower heart rate and core temperature at a given workload, earlier and greater sweating, and lower perceived effort. Performance in the heat recovers a large part of its loss. Some studies also report better performance in cool conditions, likely through plasma volume; that finding is less consistent. Acclimation can be done by training in heat or, more conveniently, by passive heating after normal training.

## Key numbers
- Duration: 10–14 days for near-complete acclimation; about 75% of the benefit within 5–7 days.
- Active protocol: 60–90 min per day at LOW to moderate intensity in 30–40 °C, or overdressed/indoors with minimal fan, aiming for a clearly elevated core temperature and heavy sweating.
- Passive protocols after a normal session: hot bath at about 40 °C for 20–40 min, or sauna at 80–90 °C for 20–30 min, on 5–10 consecutive or near-consecutive days.
- Reported effects: plasma volume +5–15%; heart rate at fixed work −10–15 beats; time-trial performance in heat improved by about 5–8%; one well-known study also found about 6% improvement in cool conditions and +5% VO2max in trained cyclists.
- Decay: roughly 2.5% per day without exposure; mostly retained for 1–2 weeks; re-acclimation is faster (2–4 days). One exposure every 3–5 days maintains it.
- Timing: finish the block 1–3 days before the event, tapering the heat load.

## In practice
- Heat sessions add stress: reduce training intensity during the block and keep key intervals in cool conditions.
- Rehydrate with sodium after each exposure; weigh before and after early sessions.
- Start with shorter exposures; stop if dizzy, nauseous or unwell.
- Not for anyone with heart disease, during illness, or in pregnancy without medical advice. Never use hot baths or sauna alone when exhausted.

## Limits
Protocols vary and individual responses differ; cool-weather performance benefits are debated.

## Coach guidance
Suggest acclimation for any important event expected above about 25 °C, with a 10–14 day lead time and explicit safety rules.
