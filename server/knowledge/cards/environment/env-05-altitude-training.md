## Summary
Living at moderate altitude for several weeks stimulates red-blood-cell production, raising haemoglobin mass and with it the oxygen-carrying capacity that underlies VO2max. The classic approach is "live high, train low": sleep high for the stimulus, do hard sessions lower to keep training quality. It is standard among professionals. The physiological response is well documented on average; the size of the performance benefit at sea level, and how much of it is due to the altitude itself rather than the camp, is still debated, and individuals respond very differently.

## Key numbers
- Effective dose: living at about 2000–2500 m for 3–4 weeks, at least 12–14 h per day at altitude.
- Haemoglobin mass: increases by roughly 1% per 100 h of exposure in pooled data, i.e. about 3–5% from a typical camp.
- Performance gain at sea level in positive studies: about 1–3%; some well-controlled studies in elite athletes found none.
- Iron: ferritin should be adequate before going (commonly taken as above about 30–50 µg/L); iron supplementation during camps is a medical decision.
- First week at altitude: reduce training load by about 20–30%; hard sessions at lower altitude or with lower power targets and longer recoveries.
- Return to sea level: best performances are often reported in the first 2–4 days or again after about 2–3 weeks, with a flat period between; individual and poorly predictable.
- Benefit fades over 3–6 weeks.

## In practice
- For amateurs, a week in the mountains is a great training camp but too short for a blood response; its value is volume, climbing and enjoyment.
- Illness, poor sleep and under-eating cancel the benefit: eat more carbohydrate, drink more, prioritise sleep.
- Altitude tents and simulated altitude require the same hours of exposure and medical oversight of iron status.
- Heat training is being studied as an alternative route to higher haemoglobin mass; evidence is emerging.

## Limits
Responses vary from none to large; blinding is nearly impossible, so placebo and camp effects are hard to separate.

## Coach guidance
Do not present altitude camps as necessary for amateurs. If a rider plans one, help with load reduction in week one and refer iron questions to a doctor.
