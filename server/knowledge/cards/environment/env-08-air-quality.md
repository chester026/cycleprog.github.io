## Summary
During hard exercise a rider breathes 10–20 times more air than at rest, mostly through the mouth, so the dose of pollutants rises sharply. Fine particles, ozone and nitrogen dioxide irritate the airways, can reduce performance on bad days and carry long-term health risks. For most people in most cities the health benefits of regular cycling clearly outweigh the pollution harm; the practical task is to reduce exposure on bad days and in bad places, not to stop riding.

## Key numbers
- Air Quality Index guide (US scale): 0–50 good; 51–100 acceptable, sensitive people may notice; 101–150 reduce prolonged or hard outdoor efforts if sensitive; 151–200 move hard sessions indoors, keep outdoor rides short and easy; above 200 avoid outdoor training.
- Fine particles (PM2.5): traffic, heating, wildfire smoke; highest near busy roads and in winter inversions.
- Ozone: forms in sunlight; peaks on hot afternoons; lowest in the early morning.
- Exposure falls steeply with distance from traffic: a parallel side street or path often has substantially lower levels than the main road.
- Modelling studies suggest that only at very high pollution levels, sustained for long daily durations, would harm outweigh the benefit of active travel.

## In practice
- Check the local air-quality index or app before long or hard rides.
- Ride early in the day in summer (ozone); avoid rush hours and heavy-traffic corridors; choose parks, bike paths and routes upwind of traffic.
- On poor days: indoor session with windows closed (and an air purifier if available), or an easy shorter ride.
- Wildfire smoke: treat seriously; move training indoors; well-fitted filtering masks help for commuting but are impractical for hard efforts.
- Symptoms such as cough, chest tightness, wheeze or unusual breathlessness mean stop and reassess; persistent symptoms need a doctor.
- Riders with asthma, heart or lung disease should follow their medical plan and use lower thresholds.

## Limits
Performance effects of moderate pollution are small and inconsistent in studies; long-term risk estimates come from population data, not athletes.

## Coach guidance
When air quality is poor, suggest swapping the day's session rather than cancelling. Keep the message proportionate.
