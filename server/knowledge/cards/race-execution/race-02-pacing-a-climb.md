## Summary
On a climb, speed follows power almost linearly, drafting matters little, and the price of starting too hard is paid on the same slope. The reliable approach is to set a power ceiling from the climb's expected duration, hold steady or build slightly, and let gradient changes alter speed and gear rather than effort.

## Key numbers
| Climb duration | Single climb, maximal | One of several in a long event |
|---|---|---|
| 3–8 min | 110–120% FTP | 95–105% |
| 10–20 min | 100–108% | 88–95% |
| 20–40 min | 95–102% | 80–90% |
| 40–90 min | 88–95% | 75–85% |

- Start: first 2–3 min at or 2–3% below target; never above.
- Steeper pitches within a climb: up to +5–8% for sections under 2 min; ease on flatter parts only enough to recover, since speed is higher there and time loss smaller.
- Cadence: 70–90 rpm; a gear that allows at least 65–70 rpm on the steepest sustained part.
- Altitude above about 1500 m: reduce targets by about 1% per 100 m.
- Late in long events: reduce targets by 5–10% for fatigue (see `load-12-durability-metrics`).
- Heart rate: use as a ceiling on long climbs; it should stabilise within 5–8 min, not keep climbing.

## In practice
- Eat and drink before the climb and on gentler sections; little is absorbed at maximal effort.
- In a group, holding a wheel still helps on gradients under about 5% and into headwind.
- Riding one's own pace and letting faster climbers go is usually faster overall than following and blowing up.
- Over the top: keep pressure on for 20–30 s to gain speed before recovering.

## Limits
Targets assume a current FTP and cool conditions; heat lowers sustainable power by several percent.

## Coach guidance
For each key climb give duration estimate, power range and a starting cap. Praise disciplined starts.
