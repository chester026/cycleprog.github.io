## Summary
Pacing is the distribution of effort over an event. For efforts longer than a few minutes on a uniform course, even power is close to optimal, because both starting too fast and saving too much cost time. On real courses the fastest strategy shifts some power toward the slow sections, climbs and headwinds, and away from fast sections, because time is lost where speed is low. Very short events are different: they reward a fast start.

## Key numbers
- Under about 60–90 s: all-out from the start.
- About 2–5 min: fast start (first 15–30 s well above average), then hold; slight positive split is normal.
- Longer than about 5 min, flat and calm: within ±2–3% of target power; the last 10% can rise.
- Variable terrain: about 5–10% above average target on climbs and into headwinds, 5–10% below on descents and with tailwinds. Modelling suggests time savings of 1–3% over strictly constant power, provided surges stay below the level that drains W′ irrecoverably.
- Cost of a fast start in efforts over 10 min: 5% too hard in the first quarter typically costs more than it gains through an earlier fade.
- Perceived effort should rise steadily: about 60–70% of maximal RPE in the first third of a time trial, maximal only at the end.

## In practice
- Decide target power from recent comparable efforts, adjusted for heat, altitude and fatigue.
- Use lap average power, not instantaneous power, to judge pace.
- The first minutes always feel too easy because oxygen uptake lags; trust the number.
- In mass-start events tactics override ideal pacing; spend effort where it buys position or a draft.

## Limits
Optimal variable pacing depends on accurate knowledge of course and wind; larger variations than about 10% push riders above critical power for too long.

## Coach guidance
Give one target number, one rule for hills and wind, and one rule for the start.
