## Summary
A taper reduces training load before a key event so that fatigue disappears faster than fitness. The evidence is unusually clear: cut volume substantially, keep intensity, keep frequency roughly the same. A meta-analysis of 27 studies in competitive runners, swimmers and cyclists found the best results with about two weeks of progressively reduced volume. The gain is modest in percent but decisive in a race.

## Key numbers
- Duration: about 2 weeks (range 8–14 days works for most amateurs; up to 3 weeks after very heavy training).
- Volume: reduce by 41–60%, progressively (larger cuts early, flattening out) rather than in one step.
- Intensity: maintain. Frequency: maintain or reduce by at most about 20%.
- Expected benefit: about 2–3% in performance (range 0–6%).
- Riders on low volume (under about 6 h/week): 5–7 days and a 30–40% cut are enough.
- CTL typically falls 5–10%; TSB rises to about +5 to +20 on race day.

## A two-week example (10 h/week rider)
- Week −2: 6–6.5 h; two quality sessions with normal intensity and about 60–70% of usual work time; long ride 2.5 h.
- Week −1: 4–4.5 h; one short quality session early (e.g. 3 × 4 min VO2max or 2 × 8 min threshold); 60–90 min easy rides; day −2 off or 30 min; day −1 "openers": 45 min easy with 3–4 × 1 min hard.

## In practice
- Feeling sluggish or restless in the first days is common and not a sign of lost fitness.
- Keep carbohydrate intake up as volume drops in the final 2–3 days; avoid large weight-loss efforts.
- Do not add new sessions, equipment or positions in the taper.
- A taper only works if there was real load before it.

## Limits
Optimal duration is individual; some riders perform best with a shorter, lighter taper. Evidence for events longer than about 4–5 h is thinner.

## Coach guidance
Hold the rider back from extra training in the final week and normalise taper feelings.
