## Summary
Alternating hard and easy days is the oldest rule in endurance training and remains well supported: hard sessions disturb the body for a day or two, easy sessions barely do, and day-to-day variation in load is itself protective. Observational work links high training monotony (similar load every day) combined with high total load to illness and overreaching. The aim is to make hard days hard enough to matter and easy days easy enough to recover.

## Key numbers
- Autonomic recovery after sessions above the first threshold: delayed for hours in trained athletes; after LOW sessions: minutes.
- Glycogen restoration after a depleting session: about 24 h with adequate carbohydrate, up to 48 h without.
- Recovery to full interval quality: typically 48 h; 72 h for masters riders or after very hard sessions.
- Monotony index (weekly mean daily load / SD): aim under about 1.5–2.0.

## Patterns
- Standard: hard – easy – hard – easy – long – easy – off.
- Back-to-back hard days (block style): day 1 the higher-intensity session (VO2max), day 2 the lower-intensity one (threshold or long tempo), then 2 easy days. Useful for stage-race preparation and for riders with two free days.
- Long ride the day after intervals is fine if it stays LOW; intervals the day after a depleting long ride usually suffer.

## In practice
- If the second hard session of the week is regularly below target, the day between is not easy enough or not long enough.
- Easy days include easy life: a heavy work day plus poor sleep is not recovery.
- Deliberate stacking (camps, stage simulation) needs a planned unloading afterwards.

## Limits
Monotony research is observational and partly from other sports. Individual recovery speeds differ widely.

## Coach guidance
When a rider strings several moderately hard days together, describe the pattern and the alternative; do not criticise any single ride.
