## Summary
Three ways to arrange the same ingredients. Traditional periodization moves gradually from high volume/low intensity to lower volume/higher intensity, mixing abilities each week. Block periodization concentrates one type of work (often high-intensity sessions) into short blocks, then maintains it. Reverse periodization starts with intensity and adds volume later. Small trials and one meta-analysis suggest block concentration of HIGH sessions gives slightly better gains in trained athletes; the evidence is limited and systematic reviews in cyclists find no clear winner.

## Key numbers
- Classic block trial in trained cyclists: 4 weeks, 8 HIGH sessions in both groups. Block group: 5 sessions in week 1, then 1 per week; traditional: 2 per week. Block group gained more VO2max (+4.6%) and power at 2 mmol/L (+10%), with no significant change in the traditional group (10 + 9 riders).
- Meta-analysis: small favourable effects of block periodization on VO2max and peak power (standardized differences of about 0.4 and 0.3), from few small studies of low methodological quality.
- Typical block: 5–7 days with 4–5 hard sessions, followed by 5–10 days of mainly LOW riding with 1 maintenance session.
- Reverse periodization trials: no consistent advantage.

## In practice
- A HIGH block suits trained riders who have plateaued, training camps and time-limited weeks; follow it with a genuinely easy week.
- Not for beginners, riders with poor sleep or high life stress, or masters riders without long recovery.
- Reverse order is practical in winter: short hard indoor sessions first, long rides when weather allows.
- Traditional mixed weeks remain the default for most amateurs.

## Limits
Studies are short (4–12 weeks), small, mostly from one group; block weeks also change intensity distribution and total load, so the cause of the benefit is uncertain.

## Coach guidance
Offer block weeks as an occasional tool with a clear recovery plan. Do not present any model as proven best.
