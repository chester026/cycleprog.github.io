## Summary
A plateau is a period of several weeks in which performance markers stop improving although training continues. It is a normal part of training, more frequent the longer someone has trained, and it has a short list of usual causes: the stimulus has stopped being an overload, recovery is insufficient, or the measurement is hiding progress. The first step is to check that the plateau is real; the second is to decide whether the rider needs more stimulus or more recovery, because the two call for opposite changes.

## Key numbers
- Call it a plateau only after about 6–8 weeks without a change beyond measurement noise (about 3% in power; see `load-15-progress-vs-noise`).
- Expected rates slow with training age: 10–25% a year in threshold power for first-year riders, 5–10% in years 1–3, 1–4% later. A long-trained rider gaining 2% in a season is progressing.
- A given workout stops being an overload after roughly 3–6 repetitions unless it is progressed.
- In a dose study, people who did not improve on 1–2 sessions a week all improved when two sessions a week were added.

## Step 1 — is it real?
- Has a maximal effort at that duration actually been attempted recently? No attempt, no new best.
- Same power meter, similar conditions (heat lowers power without loss of fitness)?
- Did something else improve: durability, power at a given heart rate, repeatability, longer rides?

## Step 2 — more stimulus or more recovery?
- **Signs of too little stimulus**: sessions feel routine, same workouts for months, flat weekly hours, heart rate and RPE normal or low, good sleep and mood.
- **Signs of too little recovery**: sessions feel harder than they should, power fading in late intervals, heart rate sluggish, poor sleep, low motivation, frequent colds, high life stress, eating too little.

## What to change (one thing at a time, for 4–6 weeks)
- Too little stimulus: add volume (15–20% more weekly time, mostly LOW); or change the type of intensity (threshold block → VO2max block, or the reverse); or progress the workout itself (see `wko-17-progressing-a-workout`); or add heavy strength training.
- Too little recovery: an easy week now; then fewer hard days (2 instead of 3), easier easy days, more sleep and carbohydrate.
- Either way: a new goal or event, a test in different form (a climb, a time trial), or a short break if the rider is stale.

## Limits
Guidance is coaching practice; individual ceilings and response rates differ widely, and at some point gains become small for everyone. Persistent decline with fatigue is not a plateau and needs the checks in `rec-02-overreaching`.

## Coach guidance
Look at the rider's data before answering: weekly hours, session mix and markers over the last 8–12 weeks. Say first what has held or improved, then name the most likely cause and propose one change with a time frame. Never present a plateau as a failure or as a lack of talent.
