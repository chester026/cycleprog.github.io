## Summary
Short intervals string together many brief hard efforts with even briefer recoveries, so oxygen uptake stays high across a whole set while power is higher than in a continuous effort. In effort-matched comparisons in trained and elite cyclists, a 30/15 format produced better performance gains than 4 × 5 min. It is an alternative route to the same goal as classic VO2max intervals, not a replacement for all of them.

## Key numbers
- Classic protocol: 3 sets of 13 × 30 s hard / 15 s easy, 3 min between sets (19.5 min of work).
- Power: about 115–130% FTP on the work phases, about 50% on recoveries; paced as the highest power sustainable across all sets.
- Elite cyclists (VO2max about 73 mL/kg/min), 9 sessions in 3 weeks, 9 riders per group: 20-min power +4.7% with short intervals vs −1.4% with 4 × 5 min; peak aerobic power +3.7% vs −0.3%; no difference in VO2max change.
- Acute comparison in elite cyclists: about 14% higher mean power and more time above 90% VO2max in the short-interval session at equal effort.
- Other formats: 40/20 × 8–10 per set; 20/10; 15/15 for newer riders.

## Why it works
Recoveries are too short for oxygen uptake to fall much, so a set behaves like one long effort at high VO2 with fluctuating power, allowing higher mechanical work and muscle activation at similar perceived strain.

## In practice
- Easier to execute on a trainer or steady climb; use lap alerts.
- Mentally more manageable for many riders than long reps.
- Specific for criteriums, cyclocross, mountain biking and punchy road racing.

## Limits
Evidence comes from one research group and small samples; comparison protocols were specific. Short recoveries are essential: 30/30 at the same power is a much easier session.

## Coach guidance
Offer as a variation after a block of longer VO2max intervals or for riders who struggle with long reps. Analyse by set average and by fade across sets.
