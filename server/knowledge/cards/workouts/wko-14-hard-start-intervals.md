## Summary
Oxygen uptake rises faster when an interval starts above its average power. Hard-start intervals and intervals with power surges exploit this: a brief burst at the beginning (or repeatedly within the rep) and then a lower, steadier power. Acute studies in well-trained athletes show more time near VO2max than in evenly paced intervals of the same mean power, at similar perceived effort. Whether this produces larger training gains over weeks is not yet established.

## Key numbers
- Hard-start: first 60–90 s at 120–130% FTP, then the remaining 3–4 min at 100–108%.
- Varied-power (Bossi-type): 5-min reps with 3 × 30-s surges at about 100% of ramp peak power, separated by 1 min at about 77%; mean power equal to an even-paced rep at about 84% of peak.
- Reported result in well-trained cyclists: roughly 40–45% more time above 90% VO2max per session than constant-power reps (410 s versus 286 s).
- Sessions: 4–6 reps of 5 min, recovery 2.5–3 min.

## Why it works
Faster oxygen-uptake kinetics after a high-power onset, and greater recruitment of fast fibres, bring VO2 to its peak earlier in each repetition.

## In practice
- Use as a progression for riders who already tolerate 5 × 5 min evenly paced.
- The trailing power must still be above threshold; if it falls below about 100% FTP, lower the starting burst.
- Mimics racing: attack, then sustain.

## Limits
Mostly acute studies with small samples; individual responses vary. Poorly judged starts end the interval early.

## Coach guidance
Present as an advanced variation with promising acute evidence. In analysis, a deliberate fast start followed by steady power above threshold is correct execution, not a pacing error.
