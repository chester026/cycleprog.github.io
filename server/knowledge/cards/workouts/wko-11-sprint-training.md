## Summary
Maximal sprints of 5–15 seconds train peak power: the nervous system's ability to recruit muscle fully and quickly. They cost little metabolically when recoveries are long, so they can be added to easy rides without turning them into hard days. Studies in elite cyclists indicate that including short sprints in low-intensity rides maintains or improves sprint ability without harming endurance adaptations.

## Key numbers
- Duration: 6–12 s all-out; peak power occurs in the first 3–5 s.
- Recovery: 3–5 min very easy between sprints (phosphocreatine needs about 3+ min to restore).
- Volume: 4–10 sprints per session; 1–2 sessions per week.
- Variants: standing starts from low speed in a big gear (torque); rolling sprints at 90–110 rpm; seated high-cadence sprints (speed); small-ring sprints.
- Typical peak power: 10–14 W/kg recreational men, 15–18 W/kg trained, 20+ W/kg sprinters; women about 15–25% lower.

## In practice
- Add 4–6 sprints to an endurance ride, in the first half while fresh.
- Technique matters: gear choice, hand position in the drops, body over the bike, timing.
- Sprint training also benefits non-sprinters: closing gaps, accelerating out of corners, late-race reserve.
- Safety first: quiet, straight road, slight uphill; or the trainer.

## Limits
Power meters with 1-s recording underestimate true peaks; trainers vary in how they handle sprints. Maximal sprints stress knees and tendons in riders unaccustomed to them; build up gradually.

## Coach guidance
Suggest sprints as a low-cost addition even for endurance-focused riders, and present new 5-s bests as real progress. Skip them for riders with current knee or tendon complaints.
