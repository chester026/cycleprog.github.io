## Summary
Splitting a day's training into two sessions is mainly a scheduling tool: it lets time-limited riders add volume and lets high-volume riders spread load. A second, more specific use is "train low": doing the second session with reduced glycogen to amplify cellular signalling. Train-low studies in trained cyclists show stronger enzyme and fat-oxidation adaptations but no consistent performance advantage, and the second session's quality is lower.

## Key numbers
- Practical doubles: commute + evening ride; 60–90 min morning + 45–60 min evening; hard session in the morning, 30–60 min easy later.
- Recovery between: 4–8 h with a carbohydrate-containing meal (1–1.2 g/kg/h for the first hours) if the second session needs quality.
- Train-low design: hard or long session in the morning, little carbohydrate afterwards, easy 45–75 min in the afternoon or next morning fasted.
- In a 3-week study of trained cyclists, twice-every-second-day training raised glycogen and fat-oxidation markers more than daily training, yet 60-min time-trial gains were similar (about 10% in both), and interval power in the low-glycogen sessions was lower.
- Norwegian-style double threshold: two controlled threshold sessions in one day, used by elite runners and triathletes.

## In practice
- For most amateurs, one longer ride beats two short ones for durability; doubles are for when a long ride is impossible.
- Keep only one of the two sessions hard.
- Use train-low sparingly (once a week at most) and only on easy second sessions.

## Limits
Low-glycogen training increases perceived effort, illness risk and, if frequent, the risk of low energy availability. Double-threshold evidence in cyclists is anecdotal.

## Coach guidance
Recommend doubles for logistics, not as a superior method. Do not suggest train-low to riders with signs of under-fuelling, heavy fatigue or weight-loss focus.
