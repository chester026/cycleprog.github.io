## Summary
Cadence drills, such as spin-ups, high-cadence intervals and single-leg pedalling, aim at coordination: a smooth stroke across a wide cadence range. They are harmless, cost little and help newer riders who are locked into one cadence. Evidence that they improve efficiency or performance in trained cyclists is lacking; deliberately "pulling up" on the pedals actually lowers gross efficiency in studies.

## Key numbers
- Spin-ups: 6–10 × 20–30 s building to 110–130+ rpm in an easy gear, stopping when bouncing on the saddle; 1–2 min easy between.
- High-cadence endurance: 3–5 × 5 min at 100–110 rpm at LOW power.
- Single-leg drills (trainer): 4–6 × 30–60 s per leg at low resistance.
- Freely chosen cadence in trained cyclists: usually 85–100 rpm on the flat, 70–85 rpm climbing.

## In practice
- Most useful for beginners, riders who habitually grind below 75 rpm, and track or criterium riders who need leg speed.
- Place in warm-ups or easy rides; they do not count as hard work.
- The ability to change cadence freely is valuable in racing: accelerations are faster from a higher cadence.
- Do not coach riders to pull up or to pedal "circles"; cue a relaxed upper body and a quiet saddle instead.

## Limits
Experienced cyclists already pedal near their individually economical pattern; changing it by instruction rarely helps. Power-meter metrics such as pedal smoothness and torque effectiveness have not been shown to predict performance.

## Coach guidance
Offer drills as light variety and skill work. Do not interpret left/right balance or smoothness scores as problems unless there is pain or a large, consistent asymmetry (then suggest a bike fit or physiotherapist).
