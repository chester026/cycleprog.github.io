## Summary
What riders say to themselves during hard efforts influences how hard the effort feels and how long they sustain it. Planned, practised self-talk is among the best-supported mental skills in sport: a meta-analysis found a moderate positive effect on performance, and in a cycling study two weeks of practising motivational self-talk lengthened time to exhaustion substantially while lowering perceived effort at the same point in the test. It costs nothing and can be trained like any other skill.

## Key numbers
- Cycling to exhaustion at 80% of peak power after a 2-week self-talk intervention: time to exhaustion increased by about 18% with lower RPE mid-effort; no change in controls (637 to 750 s; RPE at the halfway point fell from 7.3 to 6.4).
- Meta-analysis: overall effect size about 0.5 (moderate); larger with practice and for instructional self-talk in fine-skill tasks, motivational self-talk in endurance and strength tasks.

## Types
- **Motivational**: "strong and steady", "you've done this before", "one more minute".
- **Instructional**: "relax the shoulders", "smooth circles", "eyes up", "breathe out".
- Second person ("you can hold this") works at least as well as first person for many people.

## How to build it
1. Notice the automatic thoughts that appear when it gets hard ("I can't hold this", "too far to go").
2. Write two or three short replacement phrases for specific moments: the start, the middle third, the final minutes, a climb.
3. Practise them in every hard session for two weeks; keep what feels natural, drop what feels false.
4. Use cues tied to triggers: a lap beep, a kilometre marker, the top of each pedal stroke count.

## In practice
- Phrases should be believable and in the rider's own words.
- Accepting statements ("this is supposed to feel hard") often work better than denial.
- Break long efforts into segments and talk only about the current one.
- After a setback mid-ride, a reset phrase ("next section") prevents spiralling.

## Limits
Effects are larger in less experienced athletes and in lab tests than in competition; forced positivity can backfire.

## Coach guidance
Suggest it as a skill to try in the next interval session, with one or two phrases, and ask afterwards what worked.
