## Summary
In endurance exercise people rarely stop because their muscles can no longer produce the required force. They stop, or slow down, when the effort feels as high as they are willing or able to tolerate. The psychobiological model holds that perceived effort and motivation together determine endurance performance: anything that lowers the sense of effort at a given power, or raises what a person is willing to give, improves performance without any change in the body's capacity. This view complements, and is debated alongside, models centred on muscle fatigue.

## Key points
- After cycling to exhaustion at a fixed power, subjects in one study could immediately produce about three times that power in a brief sprint: exhaustion was not muscle failure.
- Perceived effort rises steadily through a hard effort; the rider stops when it reaches their maximum.
- Things that lower effort at the same power: fitness, caffeine, carbohydrate (even as a mouth rinse), cooling, sleep, music, a positive mood, encouraging self-talk, familiarity with the task.
- Things that raise it: mental fatigue, sleep loss, heat, low glycogen, anxiety, monotony, negative self-talk.
- Willingness to tolerate effort rises with meaningful goals, competition, an audience, incentives and belief that the effort is achievable.
- Effort is distinct from pain and from discomfort such as heat or breathlessness; they are regulated partly separately.

## In practice
- Training improves performance partly by making a given power feel easier and partly by teaching riders what hard, sustainable effort feels like.
- Pacing is the skill of distributing perceived effort: early restraint keeps the ceiling from arriving too soon.
- Before key sessions and events, manage the effort-raising factors: sleep, mental load, heat, fuel.
- Psychological skills (self-talk, attention, goals) work through this channel and are trainable.

## Limits
The model is contested: feedback from fatiguing muscles clearly contributes to effort and to performance limits, and the relative weight of central and peripheral factors varies with intensity and duration.

## Coach guidance
Use this to help riders see that a hard day can be about effort perception rather than lost fitness, and that perception is influenced by things they can manage.
