## Summary
People keep training for years when the activity satisfies three basic psychological needs: autonomy (it is my choice and my way), competence (I can see myself getting better at something) and relatedness (I belong with others who do this). Motivation driven by enjoyment and personal meaning lasts; motivation driven by guilt, pressure or reward alone tends to fade or turn into burnout. A systematic review of exercise studies found that more self-determined motivation consistently predicts long-term adherence.

## Key points
- Motivation types, from least to most durable: external pressure or reward → guilt and "should" → valuing the outcome (health, a goal) → identity ("I am a rider") → enjoyment of riding itself.
- Motivation fluctuates daily; that is normal and not a character flaw. Systems (schedule, habits, company) carry training through low days.
- Competence comes from visible progress on things the rider controls: completed sessions, longer rides, smoother pacing, new skills.
- Autonomy is supported by choice: between session options, routes, goals. Prescriptions without choice erode it.
- Relatedness: clubs, riding partners, shared events, even a coach who notices.
- Rewards and rankings can raise effort briefly but undermine enjoyment if they become the reason.

## In practice
- Ask why the rider rides and build the plan around that reason, not around what a plan "should" contain.
- Offer two or three ways to reach the day's purpose and let the rider pick.
- Keep some rides purely for pleasure, with no targets.
- When motivation dips, look first for fatigue, monotony, life stress or a goal that no longer fits (see `psy-11-burnout`).
- A short, easy "just start" ride often restores momentum; ten minutes in, most people continue.
- Variety, new routes and new skills refresh motivation.

## Limits
Theory explains tendencies, not individuals; some riders thrive on competition and numbers, others are put off by them.

## Coach guidance
Support the rider's own reasons and choices. Avoid guilt, pressure and comparison with others as levers. Notice and name progress that the rider can attribute to their own actions.
