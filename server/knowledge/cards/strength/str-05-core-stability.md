## Summary
The trunk is the platform the legs push against. Evidence that core training directly raises cycling power is thin, but there is reasonable support for the view that a fatigued trunk alters pedalling mechanics, and lower-back pain is among the most common complaints in cyclists. A small, regular dose of trunk work is cheap insurance for comfort on long rides and for holding low positions, and it complements heavy lifting.

## Key numbers
- Dose: 10–15 min, 2–3 times per week, or appended to gym sessions.
- Exercises (pick 4–5): front plank and side plank (3 × 20–45 s), dead bug, bird dog, glute bridge and single-leg bridge, Pallof press (anti-rotation), back extension, hanging or lying knee raise.
- Progression: longer holds up to about 60 s, then harder variations or added load; quality of position over duration.
- In a small study, fatiguing the core changed knee and ankle mechanics during cycling without changing power in the short term.

## In practice
- Heavy compound lifts (squat, deadlift) already load the trunk substantially.
- Prioritise for riders with back discomfort on long rides, time-trialists and riders adapting to a lower position.
- Include hip and glute strength: weak hip stabilisers contribute to knee tracking problems.
- Short daily routines are easier to sustain than long weekly ones.
- Back pain that persists off the bike, radiates into the leg or comes with numbness needs medical or physiotherapy assessment; check bike fit as well (see `bike-08-bike-fit`).

## Limits
Direct performance evidence is weak; much of the rationale is clinical experience. "Core stability" is loosely defined across studies.

## Coach guidance
Offer a simple routine when comfort or position is the issue. Do not present core work as a performance booster or as treatment for an injury.
