## Summary
Cycling holds the body in a flexed position through a limited range of motion for hours. Riders do not need exceptional flexibility; they need enough hip, hamstring and thoracic mobility to sit comfortably in their position and to function well off the bike. Stretching does not prevent soreness or speed recovery, and its effect on injury risk in cycling is unproven. Its real use is to maintain range of motion and comfort.

## Key numbers
- Static stretching: 30–60 s per muscle group, 2–3 repetitions, most days, increases range of motion over weeks.
- Long static stretches (over 60 s per muscle) immediately before maximal efforts can reduce power for a short time; short stretches within a full warm-up have negligible effect.
- Strength training through full range improves flexibility about as well as stretching.
- Typical tight areas in cyclists: hip flexors, quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes/piriformis, calves, chest and neck; stiff thoracic spine.
- Minimal routine: 8–10 min, 3–5 times per week.

## A basic routine
Hip-flexor lunge stretch, hamstring stretch (lying, straight leg), figure-four glute stretch, calf stretch, thoracic extension over a roller or chair back, doorway chest stretch, cat–cow and child's pose, neck side-bend.

## In practice
- Best done after riding or in the evening, when muscles are warm.
- Riders who cannot hold a low position comfortably should test hamstring and hip mobility before blaming fitness; position can also be adapted to the body.
- Desk workers benefit from standing and moving every 30–60 min more than from one long stretching session.
- Yoga or Pilates once a week covers mobility and trunk control together.
- Sharp pain, pins and needles or pain that persists is not a stretching matter; see a physiotherapist.

## Limits
Evidence linking flexibility to cycling performance or injury is weak; more range is not always better.

## Coach guidance
Suggest a short routine for comfort and position, without promising performance or injury prevention.
