## Summary
Adding heavy strength training to normal endurance training improves cycling performance in trained riders: time-trial power, power at the end of long rides, sprint power and, in several studies, efficiency. Reviews conclude that for cyclists the evidence is strongest for heavy loads rather than explosive or light, high-repetition work. Body mass typically does not increase when lifting is combined with substantial endurance training.

## Key numbers
- Typical programme in the trials: 2 sessions per week for 10–12 weeks, 4–10 repetition-maximum loads, 2–3 sets, lower-body exercises.
- Reported gains in well-trained cyclists: mean power in a 40–45 min time trial up by about 4–7%; 5-min all-out power after 3 h of riding up by about 7% versus no change in controls; peak power in sprints and maximal aerobic power up by several percent (approximate, from memory).
- Maximal leg strength: +20–35% in previously non-lifting cyclists.
- VO2max: unchanged by strength training.
- Thigh muscle size: small increases (a few percent) without gain in total body mass.

## Why it works
Stronger type I fibres work at a lower fraction of their maximum, delaying recruitment of less efficient type II fibres; conversion of the fastest fibres toward more fatigue-resistant ones; higher rate of force development, so peak force arrives earlier in the pedal stroke; possibly stiffer tendons. The benefit is largest late in long efforts and in sprints.

## In practice
- Most valuable for: long-event riders (durability), racers (final sprint, repeated efforts), masters riders (muscle and bone), anyone with a history of only riding.
- Light circuits and bodyweight endurance sets do not produce these effects; the load must be heavy relative to maximum.
- Bone health is an additional reason: cycling alone does not load bones.

## Limits
Studies are small and several come from one research group; not all find efficiency gains in highly trained riders; initial soreness can disturb bike sessions for 2–3 weeks.

## Coach guidance
Recommend strength work as a standard part of training, with technique learned first. Riders with injuries or no lifting experience should start with qualified instruction.
