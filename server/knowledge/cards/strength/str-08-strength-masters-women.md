## Summary
The case for lifting is strongest in two groups. From about age 40 muscle mass, maximal strength and especially power decline year by year, faster after 60, and endurance training alone does not stop it; bone density also falls, and cycling does not load bone. Women respond to heavy strength training with performance gains similar to men's and have additional reason to protect bone, particularly around and after menopause. For both groups strength work is a health measure as much as a performance one.

## Key numbers
- Age-related loss without resistance training: muscle mass roughly 3–8% per decade after 30–40, strength and power faster (about 1–3% per year after 50–60).
- Masters cyclists: 3 weeks of strength training improved cycling efficiency and maximal strength in a study of master endurance athletes, removing the initial gap to younger riders (9 masters, 8 young; maximal strength +18% in masters).
- Female cyclists: 11 weeks of heavy leg strength training (2 sessions/week) improved 40-min time-trial power, efficiency and the fraction of VO2max used, in a trial of 19 riders.
- Recommended frequency for masters: 2 sessions per week year-round; in-season at least 1.
- Protein: 0.4 g/kg per meal (about 30–40 g) to support the response in older riders.
- Recovery: allow 48–72 h between a heavy session and key bike intervals for riders over 50.

## In practice
- Same exercises and principles as for anyone (see `str-02-exercises-loading`); progress load more gradually and prioritise technique and joint comfort.
- Include some fast, power-oriented movements (light jumps, fast-intent lifts) since power declines faster than strength.
- Add balance and single-leg work for older riders.
- Women will not "bulk up" on two sessions a week combined with endurance training.
- Low bone density, prior fractures, joint replacement or cardiovascular conditions call for medical clearance and supervised programming.

## Limits
Trials in masters and female cyclists are few and small; long-term outcome data come mostly from general ageing research.

## Coach guidance
Give strength training higher priority in plans for masters riders and treat it as non-negotiable for long-term riding health, in a friendly, non-alarmist tone.
